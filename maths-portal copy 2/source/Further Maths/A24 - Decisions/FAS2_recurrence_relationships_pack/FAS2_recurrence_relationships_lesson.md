# 4. Learning Objectives

## Core Further Maths objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain what it means for a process to be **recursive**.
2. Identify a **recurrence relation** and its **initial condition** or initial conditions.
3. Generate terms from a recurrence relation.
4. Form recurrence relations from written contexts.
5. Distinguish between a **recursive form** and a **closed form**.
6. Verify that a proposed closed form satisfies a recurrence relation.
7. Solve first-order homogeneous recurrence relations of the form \(u_n=a u_{n-1}\).
8. Solve first-order non-homogeneous recurrence relations of the form \(u_n=a u_{n-1}+g(n)\).

10. Use initial conditions to determine constants in a closed form.

In ordinary A-Level Maths, this idea appeared as sequences and series. In Further Maths, the same idea becomes more structured. A recurrence relation is not just “spot the pattern”; it is a mathematical model saying how the next state depends on earlier states. The key upgrade is that we now solve recurrence relations to find closed forms. The danger is that old sequence habits can become too casual: the index, initial condition and context all matter.

# 6. Big Picture Explanation

A recurrence relation describes a process that evolves step by step. Instead of saying directly \(u_n=\text{some formula in }n\), it may say

\[
u_{n+1}=u_n+3.
\]

This means: to get the next term, use the previous term.

Many real processes work this way. Money in a bank account depends on last month’s amount. A loan balance depends on last month’s balance. A medicine level depends on yesterday’s level. A population depends on yesterday’s population. A staircase-counting problem depends on smaller staircase-counting problems.

The big Further Maths goal is

\[
\boxed{\text{recurrence relation + initial condition(s)} \longrightarrow \text{closed form}}.
\]

For example, if

\[
u_{n+1}=u_n+3,\qquad u_0=3,
\]

then

\[
u_0=3,
\]

\[
u_1=u_0+3=3+3=6,
\]

\[
u_2=u_1+3=6+3=9.
\]

The closed form is

\[
u_n=3n+3
\]

when \(u_0=3\). If the same visible sequence \(3,6,9,\ldots\) is indexed from \(u_1\), a closed form may instead be \(u_n=3n\). Indexing matters.

# 7. Key Definitions and Notation

A **recurrence relation** is an equation that defines a term of a sequence using one or more earlier terms.

Examples:

\[
u_{n+1}=u_n+3,
\]

\[
x_n=x_{n-1}+x_{n-2}.
\]

An **initial condition** gives the starting value or starting values needed to generate the sequence.

A **recursive form** defines a term using earlier term(s). A **closed form** gives \(u_n\) directly in terms of \(n\), not in terms of earlier terms.

A recurrence relation is **first order** when the difference between the highest and lowest subscripts is \(1\). A recurrence relation is **second order** when that difference is \(2\).

A first-order recurrence relation of the form

\[
u_n=a u_{n-1}
\]

is **homogeneous**. In

\[
u_n=a u_{n-1}+g(n),
\]

\(a u_{n-1}\) is the homogeneous part and \(g(n)\) is the non-homogeneous part.

The **complementary function** is the general solution of the homogeneous part. For \(u_n=a u_{n-1}+g(n)\), the complementary function is

\[
u_n=Ca^n.
\]

A **particular solution** is one chosen solution that deals with the non-homogeneous part.

For a second-order homogeneous recurrence

\[
u_n=a u_{n-1}+b u_{n-2},
\]

the auxiliary or characteristic equation is

\[
r^2-ar-b=0.
\]

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| \(n\) | Term position or time step. |
| \(u_n\) | General sequence term. |
| \(u_0\), \(u_1\) | Initial terms. |
| \(a\) | Constant multiplier in a first-order recurrence. |
| \(g(n)\) | Function of \(n\) in a non-homogeneous recurrence. |
| \(C\) | Constant determined by initial conditions. |
| \(\lambda,\mu,\nu\) | Constants used in trial particular solutions. |
| \(r\) | Root variable in the auxiliary equation. |
| \(\alpha,\beta\) | Roots of a characteristic equation. |
| \(F_n\) | Fibonacci-type sequence term. |
| \(S_n\) | Number of ways to climb a staircase with \(n\) steps. |

# 8. Core Theory

## 8.1 What a recurrence model must contain

A recurrence model has two essential ingredients:

\[
\boxed{\text{recurrence relation}+\text{initial condition(s)}}.
\]

The recurrence relation tells you how to move from earlier terms to later terms. The initial condition tells you where the sequence starts. For example,

\[
u_{n+1}=u_n+3
\]

does not define one unique sequence until a starting value such as \(u_0=3\) is supplied.

With

\[
u_{n+1}=u_n+3,\qquad u_0=3,
\]

we get

\[
u_1=3+3=6,
\]

\[
u_2=6+3=9,
\]

\[
u_3=9+3=12.
\]

**Bridge Note:** In ordinary A-Level Maths, you often described this as “add 3 each time”. Here, Further Maths turns that phrase into a formal recurrence relation with an initial condition.

## 8.2 Order of a recurrence relation

For

\[
u_n=5u_{n-1},
\]

the order is

\[
n-(n-1)=1.
\]

For

\[
x_n=x_{n-1}+x_{n-2},
\]

the order is

\[
n-(n-2)=2.
\]

The Fibonacci-type relation needs two starting values:

\[
x_0=1,\qquad x_1=1.
\]

Then

\[
x_2=x_1+x_0=1+1=2,
\]

\[
x_3=x_2+x_1=2+1=3,
\]

\[
x_4=x_3+x_2=3+2=5.
\]

## 8.3 Recursive form versus closed form

Recursive form:

\[
u_n=u_{n-1}+3.
\]

Closed form:

\[
u_n=3n+1.
\]

The recurrence relation \(u_n=u_{n-1}+3\) describes a family of sequences unless an initial condition is supplied. The closed form \(u_n=3n+1\) describes one particular sequence.

If \(u_1=4\), then

\[
4=3(1)+K,
\]

so \(K=1\), and

\[
u_n=3n+1.
\]

If \(u_0=3\), then

\[
3=3(0)+K,
\]

so \(K=3\), and

\[
u_n=3n+3.
\]

## 8.4 Verifying that a closed form satisfies a recurrence relation

To verify \(u_n=3n+1\) satisfies \(u_n=u_{n-1}+3\), first find

\[
u_{n-1}=3(n-1)+1=3n-3+1=3n-2.
\]

Then

\[
u_{n-1}+3=(3n-2)+3=3n+1=u_n.
\]

To verify \(u_n=2\cdot 3^{n-1}\) satisfies \(u_n=3u_{n-1}\), find

\[
u_{n-1}=2\cdot 3^{n-2}.
\]

Then

\[
3u_{n-1}=3(2\cdot 3^{n-2})=2\cdot 3^1\cdot 3^{n-2}=2\cdot 3^{n-1}=u_n.
\]

**Bridge Note:** This is substitution, expansion and simplification, familiar from ordinary Maths. The Further Maths upgrade is that you substitute \(n-1\), not just a number.

## 8.5 Forming first-order recurrence relations from context

For a car loan of £8000 with monthly interest \(3\%\), followed by a £500 repayment, let

\[
u_n=\text{balance remaining after }n\text{ months}.
\]

Then

\[
u_n=1.03u_{n-1}-500,\qquad u_0=8000.
\]

The model may eventually produce \(u_n<0\), which does not make sense for a loan balance.

For a drug dosage model where \(90\%\) is lost each day and \(125\) mg is added, let

\[
d_n=\text{amount of drug after }n\text{ days}.
\]

Then

\[
d_n=0.1d_{n-1}+125.
\]

The initial condition depends on whether \(d_0\) is measured before or after the first dose.

The limiting amount \(L\) satisfies

\[
L=0.1L+125,
\]

so

\[
0.9L=125,
\]

and

\[
L=\frac{1250}{9}\text{ mg}.
\]

## 8.6 Forming second-order recurrence relations from context

For bacteria growth with \(b_0=200\), \(b_1=220\), and increase doubling each hour, the current increase is

\[
b_n-b_{n-1},
\]

and the previous increase is

\[
b_{n-1}-b_{n-2}.
\]

Since the increase doubles,

\[
b_n-b_{n-1}=2(b_{n-1}-b_{n-2}).
\]

Expanding gives

\[
b_n-b_{n-1}=2b_{n-1}-2b_{n-2},
\]

so

\[
\boxed{b_n=3b_{n-1}-2b_{n-2},\qquad b_0=200,\quad b_1=220.}
\]

For the staircase-counting model, let \(S_n\) be the number of ways to climb \(n\) steps using one-step or two-step moves. If the first move is one step, there are \(S_{n-1}\) completions. If the first move is two steps, there are \(S_{n-2}\) completions. Hence

\[
\boxed{S_n=S_{n-1}+S_{n-2},\qquad S_1=1,\quad S_2=2.}
\]

## 8.7 Solving first-order homogeneous recurrence relations

A first-order homogeneous recurrence relation has the form

\[
u_n=au_{n-1}.
\]

By backward substitution,

\[
u_n=a u_{n-1}=a(a u_{n-2})=a^2u_{n-2}=a^3u_{n-3}=\cdots=a^n u_0.
\]

Since \(u_0\) is a constant, write

\[
\boxed{u_n=Ca^n.}
\]

Example:

\[
x_n=2x_{n-1},\qquad x_0=3.
\]

Then

\[
x_n=C2^n.
\]

Using \(x_0=3\),

\[
3=C2^0=C,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{x_n=3\cdot 2^n.}
\]

## 8.8 Solving first-order non-homogeneous recurrence relations when \(a=1\)

For

\[
u_n=u_{n-1}+g(n),
\]

use

\[
\boxed{u_n=u_0+\sum_{r=1}^{n}g(r).}
\]

For example,

\[
u_n=u_{n-1}+n
\]

gives

\[
u_n=u_0+\sum_{r=1}^{n}r=u_0+\frac12 n(n+1).
\]

If

\[
u_n=u_{n-1}+2,\qquad u_1=5,
\]

then

\[
u_n=u_0+2n.
\]

Since \(u_1=u_0+2=5\), \(u_0=3\), so

\[
\boxed{u_n=3+2n.}
\]

## 8.9 Solving first-order non-homogeneous recurrence relations when \(a\ne1\)

For

\[
u_n=au_{n-1}+g(n),\qquad a\ne1,
\]

use:

1. solve the homogeneous part \(u_n=au_{n-1}\), giving \(u_n=Ca^n\);
2. find a particular solution for \(g(n)\);
3. add them;
4. use the initial condition to find \(C\).

| Non-homogeneous part \(g(n)\) | Try this particular solution |
|---|---|
| constant \(k\) | \(u_n=\lambda\) |
| linear \(mn+k\) | \(u_n=\lambda n+\mu\) |
| quadratic \(an^2+bn+c\) | \(u_n=\nu n^2+\lambda n+\mu\) |
| exponential \(kq^n\), where \(q\ne a\) | \(u_n=\lambda q^n\) |
| exponential \(ka^n\), same base as complementary function | \(u_n=\lambda n a^n\) |

Example:

\[
u_n=3u_{n-1}+4n,\qquad u_1=2.
\]

Complementary function:

\[
u_n=C3^n.
\]

Try

\[
u_n=\lambda n+\mu.
\]

Then

\[
u_{n-1}=\lambda(n-1)+\mu=\lambda n-\lambda+\mu.
\]

Substitute:

\[
\lambda n+
\mu=3(\lambda n-\lambda+\mu)+4n.
\]

Expand:

\[
\lambda n+
\mu=3\lambda n-3\lambda+3\mu+4n.
\]

Equating coefficients:

\[
\lambda=3\lambda+4,
\]

so

\[
\lambda=-2.
\]

For constants,

\[
\mu=-3\lambda+3\mu.
\]

Substitute \(\lambda=-2\):

\[
\mu=6+3\mu,
\]

so

\[
\mu=-3.
\]

Particular solution:

\[
u_n=-2n-3.
\]

General solution:

\[
u_n=C3^n-2n-3.
\]

Use \(u_1=2\):

\[
2=3C-2-3=3C-5,
\]

so

\[
C=\frac73.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{u_n=\frac73\,3^n-2n-3.}
\]

## 8.10 Why the multiply by \(n\) rule matters

For

\[
u_n=2u_{n-1}+3\cdot2^n,
\]

the complementary function is \(C2^n\). The trial \(\lambda2^n\) clashes with it, so use

\[
u_n=\lambda n2^n.
\]

Then

\[
u_{n-1}=\lambda(n-1)2^{n-1}.
\]

Substitute:

\[
\lambda n2^n=2[\lambda(n-1)2^{n-1}]+3\cdot2^n.
\]

This becomes

\[
\lambda n2^n=\lambda(n-1)2^n+3\cdot2^n.
\]

Divide by \(2^n\):

\[
\lambda n=\lambda(n-1)+3=\lambda n-\lambda+3.
\]

So

\[
0=-\lambda+3,
\]

and

\[
\lambda=3.
\]

## 8.11 Solving second-order homogeneous recurrence relations

For

\[
u_n=au_{n-1}+bu_{n-2},
\]

assume \(u_n=Cr^n\). Then

\[
Cr^n=aCr^{n-1}+bCr^{n-2}.
\]

Divide by \(Cr^{n-2}\):

\[
r^2=ar+b.
\]

So the auxiliary equation is

\[
\boxed{r^2-ar-b=0.}
\]

If the roots are distinct, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), then

\[
\boxed{u_n=A\alpha^n+B\beta^n.}
\]

If the root is repeated, then

\[
\boxed{u_n=(A+Bn)\alpha^n.}
\]

## 8.12 Second-order example

Solve

\[
u_n=2u_{n-1}+8u_{n-2},\qquad u_0=4,\quad u_1=10.
\]

Auxiliary equation:

\[
r^2-2r-8=0.
\]

Factorise:

\[
(r-4)(r+2)=0.
\]

So

\[
r=4\quad\text{or}\quad r=-2.
\]

General solution:

\[
u_n=A4^n+B(-2)^n.
\]

Use \(u_0=4\):

\[
4=A+B.
\]

Use \(u_1=10\):

\[
10=4A-2B.
\]

From \(B=4-A\):

\[
4A-2(4-A)=10,
\]

\[
6A-8=10,
\]

\[
A=3.
\]

Then

\[
B=1.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{u_n=3\cdot4^n+(-2)^n.}
\]

## 8.13 Fibonacci-type recurrence relations

The Fibonacci-type relation is

\[
F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}.
\]

For the classic Fibonacci sequence,

\[
F_1=1,\qquad F_2=1.
\]

The auxiliary equation is

\[
r^2-r-1=0.
\]

Using the quadratic formula,

\[
r=\frac{1\pm\sqrt5}{2}.
\]

Let

\[
\alpha=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad \beta=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}.
\]

Then

\[
F_n=A\alpha^n+B\beta^n.
\]

For the classic Fibonacci sequence,

\[
\boxed{F_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\left[\left(\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\right)^n-\left(\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}\right)^n\right].}
\]

# 11. Worked Examples

## Worked Example 1: Generate a sequence from \(u_{n+1}=u_n+3\)

Given

\[
u_{n+1}=u_n+3,\qquad u_0=3,
\]

find \(u_1,u_2,u_3,u_4\).

\[
u_1=u_0+3=3+3=6,
\]

\[
u_2=u_1+3=6+3=9,
\]

\[
u_3=u_2+3=9+3=12,
\]

\[
u_4=u_3+3=12+3=15.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{u_1=6,\quad u_2=9,\quad u_3=12,\quad u_4=15.}
\]

## Worked Example 2: Verify a closed form

Show that \(u_n=3n+1\) satisfies \(u_n=u_{n-1}+3\).

\[
u_{n-1}=3(n-1)+1=3n-3+1=3n-2.
\]

Then

\[
u_{n-1}+3=3n-2+3=3n+1=u_n.
\]

So the closed form satisfies the recurrence relation.

## Worked Example 3: Fibonacci sequence from a recurrence relation

Given

\[
x_n=x_{n-1}+x_{n-2},\qquad x_0=1,\quad x_1=1,
\]

we find

\[
x_2=x_1+x_0=1+1=2,
\]

\[
x_3=x_2+x_1=2+1=3,
\]

\[
x_4=x_3+x_2=3+2=5,
\]

\[
x_5=x_4+x_3=5+3=8.
\]

## Worked Example 4: Bank account with compound interest and deposits

Let \(a_n\) be the amount after \(n\) months. With \(2\%\) monthly interest, £100 monthly deposit, and \(a_0=500\),

\[
\boxed{a_n=1.02a_{n-1}+100,\qquad a_0=500.}
\]

Then

\[
a_1=1.02(500)+100=510+100=610,
\]

and

\[
a_2=1.02(610)+100=622.20+100=722.20.
\]

## Worked Example 5: Zombie population recurrence

Let \(P_n\) be the number of zombies on day \(n\), with \(P_0=0\), \(P_1=1\). The previous population remains, each zombie bites one human, and zombies more than one day old bite an additional three humans. Therefore

\[
P_n=P_{n-1}+P_{n-1}+3P_{n-2},
\]

so

\[
\boxed{P_n=2P_{n-1}+3P_{n-2},\qquad P_0=0,\quad P_1=1.}
\]

## Worked Example 6: Car loan recurrence and model criticism

Let \(u_n\) be the balance remaining after \(n\) months. With £8000 initial loan, \(3\%\) monthly interest and £500 repayment,

\[
\boxed{u_n=1.03u_{n-1}-500,\qquad u_0=8000.}
\]

Criticism: after a while, the model may produce \(u_n<0\), which does not make sense for a loan balance.

## Worked Example 7: Drug dosage recurrence and timing ambiguity

If \(d_n\) is the amount of drug after \(n\) days, the recurrence is

\[
\boxed{d_n=0.1d_{n-1}+125.}
\]

If \(d_0\) means immediately after the first dose, then \(d_0=125\). If \(d_0\) means before any dose, then \(d_0=0\). The question must make the timing clear.

The long-term amount satisfies

\[
L=0.1L+125,
\]

so

\[
\boxed{L=\frac{1250}{9}\text{ mg}.}
\]

## Worked Example 8: Bacteria growth as a second-order recurrence

With \(b_0=200\), \(b_1=220\), and growth increase doubling each hour:

\[
b_n-b_{n-1}=2(b_{n-1}-b_{n-2}).
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{b_n=3b_{n-1}-2b_{n-2},\qquad b_0=200,\quad b_1=220.}
\]

## Worked Example 9: Staircase recurrence

If Claudia first takes one step, there are \(S_{n-1}\) completions. If she first takes two steps, there are \(S_{n-2}\) completions. Hence

\[
\boxed{S_n=S_{n-1}+S_{n-2},\qquad S_1=1,\quad S_2=2.}
\]

# 12. Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

- Giving a recurrence relation without initial conditions.
- Confusing \(u_n\), \(u_{n-1}\) and \(u_{n+1}\).
- Treating \(u_{n-1}\) as \(u_n-1\).
- Missing brackets around \(n-1\).
- Confusing recursive form and closed form.
- Forgetting that a recurrence alone can describe many sequences.
- Using the wrong percentage multiplier: increase by \(3\%\) means \(1.03\), decrease by \(90\%\) means \(0.1\).
- Misreading “growth doubles” as “population doubles”.
- Double-counting in the zombie model.
- Forgetting the second initial condition for second-order recurrences.
- Choosing a particular solution that clashes with the complementary function.
- Writing the wrong signs in the auxiliary equation.
- Treating model criticism as optional waffle.

# 14. Worked Solutions

1. \(u_1=6\), \(u_2=10\), \(u_3=14\), \(u_4=18\).

2. \(x_2=x_1+2x_0=3+2=5\), \(x_3=x_2+2x_1=5+6=11\), \(x_4=x_3+2x_2=11+10=21\).

3. Recursive, closed form, recursive, closed form.

4. One, two, one.

5. \(u_{n+1}=u_n+5\), \(u_0=7\). Closed form \(u_n=5n+7\).

6. \(u_{n+1}=4u_n\), \(u_0=3\). Closed form \(u_n=3\cdot4^n\). Hence \(u_5=3\cdot4^5=3072\).

7. \(u_n=5+\sum_{r=1}^n2r=5+2\cdot\frac12n(n+1)=n^2+n+5\).

8. \(a_n=1.015a_{n-1}+50\), \(a_0=400\). Then \(a_1=1.015(400)+50=456\), \(a_2=1.015(456)+50=512.84\). A limitation is that the model assumes fixed interest and deposits.

9. \(d_n=0.25d_{n-1}+80\), \(d_1=80\). Then \(d_2=0.25(80)+80=100\), \(d_3=0.25(100)+80=105\). Limit \(L=0.25L+80\), so \(0.75L=80\), \(L=320/3\text{ mg}\).

10. \(u_{n-1}=2^{(n-1)+1}-3=2^n-3\). Then \(2u_{n-1}+3=2(2^n-3)+3=2^{n+1}-3=u_n\).

11. \(x_n=C(-2)^n\). Since \(x_0=5\), \(C=5\). Hence \(x_n=5(-2)^n\).

12. \(u_n=2+\sum_{r=1}^n(3r+1)=2+3\cdot\frac12n(n+1)+n=\frac32n^2+\frac52n+2\).

13. Complementary function \(C4^n\). Try \(\lambda\): \(\lambda=4\lambda+6\), so \(\lambda=-2\). General solution \(u_n=C4^n-2\). Since \(u_0=1\), \(1=C-2\), so \(C=3\). Thus \(u_n=3\cdot4^n-2\).

14. Complementary function \(C2^n\). Try \(\lambda n+\mu\). Then \(\lambda n+\mu=2(\lambda n-\lambda+\mu)+5n\). Equating coefficients gives \(\lambda=-5\), \(\mu=-10\). General solution \(u_n=C2^n-5n-10\). Since \(u_0=3\), \(C=13\). Thus \(u_n=13\cdot2^n-5n-10\).

15. \(c_0=100\), \(c_1=130\). Since increases triple, \(c_n-c_{n-1}=3(c_{n-1}-c_{n-2})\). Therefore \(c_n=4c_{n-1}-3c_{n-2}\).

16. \(R_n=R_{n-1}+R_{n-2}\), with suitable initial conditions \(R_0=1\), \(R_1=1\). Then \(R_2=2\), \(R_3=3\), \(R_4=5\), \(R_5=8\).

17. Auxiliary equation \(r^2-5r+6=0=(r-2)(r-3)\). So \(u_n=A2^n+B3^n\). Use \(u_0=4\): \(A+B=4\). Use \(u_1=7\): \(2A+3B=7\). Thus \(A=5\), \(B=-1\), and \(u_n=5\cdot2^n-3^n\).

18. Auxiliary equation \(r^2-r-1=0\). Roots \(\alpha=(1+\sqrt5)/2\), \(\beta=(1-\sqrt5)/2\). General form \(F_n=A\alpha^n+B\beta^n\). Classic closed form:

\[
F_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\left[\left(\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\right)^n-\left(\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}\right)^n\right].
\]

# 15. Exam Technique Notes

- Always define the sequence before writing the recurrence.
- State the initial condition beside the recurrence relation.
- Check whether indexing begins at \(0\) or \(1\).
- When verifying, work from the recurrence side.
- Use brackets around shifted indices such as \(n-1\).
- For \(a=1\), consider backward substitution: \(u_n=u_0+\sum_{r=1}^n g(r)\).
- For \(a\ne1\), use complementary function plus particular solution.
- For second-order recurrences, form the auxiliary equation cleanly.
- Model criticism must be specific, such as a loan balance becoming negative.
- Use exact values unless money or context asks for decimals.

## Final lesson takeaway

A recurrence relation is a rule for a mathematical time-machine:

\[
\text{previous state} \longrightarrow \text{next state}.
\]

Initial conditions tell the machine where to begin. Solving the recurrence finds the closed form, the direct formula that jumps straight to step \(n\). The whole topic is a careful balance of modelling, indexing, algebra and interpretation. Treat the subscripts like tiny gears: if one slips, the whole clock starts singing nonsense. 🕰️
