## 4. Learning Objectives

### 4.1 Core Further Maths objectives

By the end of this bridge-prerequisite lesson, you should be able to:

1. Recognise that FA22 Restitution depends on accurate before/after collision modelling.
2. Set up direct collision diagrams with consistent sign conventions.
3. Use conservation of linear momentum as the momentum equation that later combines with Newton’s law of restitution.
4. Identify why momentum alone is not enough for a full restitution problem.
5. State clearly which parts of this lesson are bridge foundations, not standalone CCEA Further Mathematics topic content.

### 4.2 Bridge objectives

You should be able to use:

\[
P=mv,
\]

\[
I=mv-mu=m(v-u),
\]

\[
I=Ft,
\]

and therefore:

\[
Ft=m(v-u).
\]

You should be able to use standard units:

\[
m\text{ in kg},\quad u,v\text{ in }m\,s^{-1},\quad F\text{ in N},\quad t\text{ in s},\quad I\text{ in Ns}.
\]

You should also be able to convert non-standard units before substitution and work with vector momentum and impulse using signs or components.

### 4.3 Exam technique objectives

You should be able to draw a before/after diagram, choose a positive direction, interpret negative answers, distinguish speed from velocity, distinguish magnitude from signed impulse, and know when to use \(I=m(v-u)\), \(I=Ft\), \(Ft=m(v-u)\), PCLM or vector components.

In ordinary A-Level Maths, this idea appeared as a way to study collisions, explosions and force-time effects using momentum and impulse.

In Further Maths, the same idea becomes the pre-wiring for restitution. Momentum conservation gives one equation, but Newton’s law of restitution supplies another equation that describes how separation and approach speeds relate.

The key upgrade is that Further Maths Restitution asks not just “is momentum conserved?”, but also “how elastic is the collision?”

The danger is treating every collision as if PCLM alone unlocks it. In FA22 Restitution, PCLM is one key. Restitution is the second key.

## 6. Big Picture Explanation

Momentum and impulse are about **change**.

A heavy object moving quickly has a large momentum. To slow it, stop it or reverse it, something must change its momentum. That “something” is called an impulse.

The supplied lesson evidence begins with collisions: sports tackles, ice hockey impacts, baseball impacts, cars crashing, and slow collisions. The point is not that all collisions are dramatic. The point is that all of them involve a change in momentum.

The main ideas are:

\[
\text{momentum}=\text{mass}\times\text{velocity}
\]

and

\[
\text{impulse}=\text{change in momentum}.
\]

So if a particle of mass \(m\) changes from initial velocity \(u\) to final velocity \(v\), then:

\[
I=mv-mu=m(v-u).
\]

If a constant force \(F\) acts for time \(t\), impulse can also be written as:

\[
I=Ft.
\]

For a collision between two particles in an isolated system:

\[
\text{total momentum before}=\text{total momentum after}.
\]

For two particles:

\[
m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2.
\]

This equation is the bridge into FA22 Restitution.

### Applied modelling context

The particles, balls, trucks, blocks and pucks in these examples are models. Common modelling assumptions include: particles have no dimensions; surfaces may be smooth; motion may be along one straight line; impact time may be short; average force may be used; and systems may be isolated during impact.

### What to watch for

Watch especially for using speed when velocity is required, forgetting unit conversions, choosing one positive direction then changing it halfway through, finding impulse on the wrong object without interpreting Newton’s third law, giving a signed impulse when the question asks for magnitude, and treating PCLM as the whole FA22 Restitution method.

## 7. Key Definitions and Notation

### 7.1 Scalars and vectors

| Quantity | Symbol | Type | Standard unit |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Initial velocity | \(u\) | Vector | \(m\,s^{-1}\) |
| Final velocity | \(v\) | Vector | \(m\,s^{-1}\) |
| Mass | \(m\) | Scalar | \(kg\) |
| Impulse | \(I\) | Vector | \(Ns\), or \(kg\,m\,s^{-1}\) |
| Force | \(F\) | Vector | \(N\) |
| Time | \(t\) | Scalar | \(s\) |
| Momentum | \(P\), or \(mv\) | Vector | \(Ns\), or \(kg\,m\,s^{-1}\) |

### 7.2 Momentum

\[
\text{Momentum}=\text{mass}\times\text{velocity},\qquad P=mv.
\]

Momentum is a vector because velocity is a vector. Its direction is the direction of the velocity.

### 7.3 Units of momentum and impulse

Momentum has units:

\[
kg\,m\,s^{-1}.
\]

Impulse has the same units, and can also be measured in newton seconds:

\[
Ns.
\]

These are equivalent because:

\[
N=kg\,m\,s^{-2},
\]

so:

\[
Ns=(kg\,m\,s^{-2})s=kg\,m\,s^{-1}.
\]

### 7.4 Impulse

Impulse is change in momentum:

\[
I=\text{final momentum}-\text{initial momentum}.
\]

So:

\[
I=mv-mu=m(v-u).
\]

If a constant force acts for time \(t\):

\[
I=Ft.
\]

Therefore:

\[
Ft=m(v-u).
\]

### 7.5 Conservation of linear momentum

For an isolated system:

\[
\text{total momentum before impact}=\text{total momentum after impact}.
\]

For two particles:

\[
m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2.
\]

### 7.6 Positive direction and magnitude

Choose one direction as positive. A negative velocity, impulse or force means the quantity acts opposite to that chosen positive direction.

The magnitude of \(-54Ns\) is:

\[
|-54|=54Ns.
\]

For a vector impulse:

\[
\mathbf{I}=a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j},
\]

the magnitude is:

\[
|\mathbf{I}|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.
\]

Speed is the magnitude of velocity.

## 8. Core Theory

### 8.1 Momentum: mass and velocity working together

Momentum measures how much “motion with direction” an object has. Larger mass gives larger momentum if velocity is unchanged. Larger velocity gives larger momentum if mass is unchanged.

\[
\boxed{P=mv}
\]

Although \(P\) can represent momentum, mechanics solutions often write the product \(mv\) directly.

**Bridge Note:** In ordinary A-Level Maths, \(F=ma\) connected a force with mass and acceleration. Momentum uses a similar product structure, but becomes part of more advanced collision models in Further Maths.

### 8.2 Momentum is a vector quantity

Mass is scalar and velocity is vector, so:

\[
\text{momentum}=m\mathbf{v}
\]

is vector. If rightwards is chosen positive, rightwards velocities are positive and leftwards velocities are negative. You do not have to choose rightwards as positive, but you must be consistent.

### 8.3 Standard units

Use kg, \(m\,s^{-1}\), N, s and Ns. Convert before substitution:

\[
300g=0.3kg,
\]

and:

\[
36km/h=\frac{36\times1000}{3600}=10m\,s^{-1}.
\]

### 8.4 Impulse as change in momentum

Impulse is something which changes the momentum of a particle. For a change from \(u\) to \(v\):

\[
I=mv-mu=m(v-u).
\]

### 8.5 Direction reversal gives larger impulse

If a \(3kg\) ball slows from \(10m\,s^{-1}\) to \(6m\,s^{-1}\) without changing direction:

\[
I=3(6-10)=-12Ns,
\]

so the magnitude is \(12Ns\).

If it rebounds at \(6m\,s^{-1}\), taking the original direction positive:

\[
I=3(-6-10)=3(-16)=-48Ns,
\]

so the magnitude is \(48Ns\). Reversing direction requires a larger impulse because the impulse first removes the original velocity and then creates velocity in the opposite direction.

### 8.6 Force-time impulse and derivation

If a constant force acts for time \(t\):

\[
I=Ft.
\]

Using \(F=ma\) and \(a=\dfrac{v-u}{t}\):

\[
F=m\left(\frac{v-u}{t}\right).
\]

Multiply by \(t\):

\[
Ft=m(v-u).
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{I=Ft=m(v-u)}.
\]

### 8.7 Braking car example

A car of mass \(600kg\) travels at \(9m\,s^{-1}\). A braking force of \(900N\) resists its motion. Find the time to come to rest.

Choose the motion direction positive:

\[
m=600,\quad u=9,\quad v=0,\quad F=-900.
\]

\[
Ft=m(v-u)
\]

\[
-900t=600(0-9)
\]

\[
-900t=-5400
\]

\[
t=6.
\]

\[
\boxed{6s}
\]

If the opposite direction were chosen positive, the force would be \(+900\) and \(u=-9\), giving the same answer. The physical result is unchanged when signs are consistent.

### 8.8 Ball falling into soil

A \(2kg\) ball falls into soil at \(15m\,s^{-1}\) and is brought to rest in \(0.4s\). Let downward be positive. The soil resistance \(R\) acts upward, so \(F=-R\).

\[
(-R)(0.4)=2(0-15)
\]

\[
-0.4R=-30
\]

\[
R=75.
\]

\[
\boxed{75N}
\]

The model uses an average/constant resistive force.

### 8.9 Stone hitting the floor

A \(3kg\) stone falls at \(18m\,s^{-1}\) and is brought to rest by the floor. Choose upward as positive, because the floor impulse is upward:

\[
u=-18,\quad v=0.
\]

\[
I=3(0-(-18))=54Ns.
\]

\[
\boxed{54Ns}
\]

If downward were chosen positive, the signed impulse would be \(-54Ns\), but the magnitude is still \(54Ns\).

### 8.10 Bullet into wood

A bullet of mass \(30g=0.03kg\) enters wood at \(400m\,s^{-1}\) and stops in \(0.02s\). Let the initial motion be positive and resistance magnitude be \(R\), so \(F=-R\):

\[
(-R)(0.02)=0.03(0-400)
\]

\[
-0.02R=-12
\]

\[
R=600.
\]

\[
\boxed{600N}
\]

### 8.11 Truck rebounding from buffers

A \(1400kg\) truck moving at \(2m\,s^{-1}\) rebounds at \(1m\,s^{-1}\). The buffer force is \(3500N\). Choose the buffer-force direction as positive:

\[
F=3500,\quad u=-2,\quad v=1.
\]

\[
3500t=1400(1-(-2))
\]

\[
3500t=4200
\]

\[
t=1.2.
\]

\[
\boxed{1.2s}
\]

The velocity change is \(1-(-2)=3\), not \(1-2\).

### 8.12 Quickfire impulse calculations

1. \(F=30N\), \(t=0.5s\):

\[
I=30\times0.5=15Ns.
\]

2. \(m=3\), \(u=10\), \(v=6\):

\[
I=3(6-10)=-12Ns,
\]

magnitude \(12Ns\).

3. \(m=3\), \(u=10\), rebound at \(6\), so \(v=-6\):

\[
I=3(-6-10)=-48Ns,
\]

magnitude \(48Ns\).

4. \(m=100\), \(u=2000\), \(v=0\):

\[
I=100(0-2000)=-200000Ns,
\]

magnitude \(200000Ns\).

5. Momentum changes from \(6\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}\) to \(10\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j}\):

\[
\mathbf{I}=(10-6)\mathbf{i}+(5-3)\mathbf{j}=4\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}.
\]

6. Momentum changes from \(6\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}\) to \(10\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j}\):

\[
\mathbf{I}=(10-6)\mathbf{i}+(5-(-5))\mathbf{j}=4\mathbf{i}+10\mathbf{j}.
\]

### 8.13 Principle of Conservation of Linear Momentum, PCLM

For an isolated system:

\[
\boxed{\text{total momentum before impact}=\text{total momentum after impact}.}
\]

For two particles:

\[
\boxed{m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2}.
\]

An isolated system experiences only internal unbalanced forces during the impact. By Newton’s third law, the impulses on the two bodies are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so total momentum is conserved.

### 8.14 Deriving conservation of momentum using impulses

Let the impulse on particle 1 be \(I\):

\[
I=m_1(v_1-u_1)=m_1v_1-m_1u_1.
\]

The impulse on particle 2 is \(-I\):

\[
-I=m_2(v_2-u_2)=m_2v_2-m_2u_2.
\]

Add:

\[
0=m_1v_1-m_1u_1+m_2v_2-m_2u_2.
\]

Rearrange:

\[
m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2.
\]

### 8.15 Collision diagrams

Draw particles, masses, initial velocities, final velocities, positive direction and impulse arrows when required. Velocities can be shown as detached arrows; impulses as attached arrows like forces.

Logical checks help: if a rear particle catches a front particle moving in the same direction, it must initially have greater velocity.

### 8.16 Example: direct collision with one unknown final velocity

A particle \(P\) of mass \(2kg\) moves at \(3m\,s^{-1}\). Particle \(Q\) of mass \(3kg\) is at rest. After collision, \(Q\) moves at \(\frac{7}{3}m\,s^{-1}\). Find \(P\)'s final velocity and the impulse received by \(P\).

Take \(P\)'s original direction as positive. Let \(P\)'s final velocity be \(v\):

\[
2(3)+3(0)=2v+3\left(\frac{7}{3}\right)
\]

\[
6=2v+7
\]

\[
2v=-1
\]

\[
v=-\frac12.
\]

So \(P\) has speed \(0.5m\,s^{-1}\) and its direction is reversed.

For the impulse received by \(P\), take the impulse direction as positive. Then \(u=-3\), \(v=0.5\), \(m=2\):

\[
I=2(0.5-(-3))=2(3.5)=7Ns.
\]

Alternatively, using \(Q\):

\[
I=3\left(\frac{7}{3}-0\right)=7Ns.
\]

### 8.17 Example: particles moving towards each other

Particles \(A\) and \(B\) have masses \(2kg\) and \(4kg\). They move towards each other with speeds \(3\) and \(2\). After collision, \(A\) reverses with speed \(2\). Take \(A\)'s original direction positive.

\[
u_A=3,\quad u_B=-2,\quad v_A=-2,\quad v_B=v.
\]

\[
2(3)+4(-2)=2(-2)+4v
\]

\[
6-8=-4+4v
\]

\[
-2=-4+4v
\]

\[
2=4v
\]

\[
v=0.5.
\]

\(B\) has speed \(0.5m\,s^{-1}\) and its direction is reversed.

Impulse on \(B\):

\[
I=4(0.5-(-2))=4(2.5)=10Ns.
\]

### 8.18 Example: light inextensible string goes taut

**Boundary warning:** This is useful bridge/enrichment evidence only. FA22 Restitution excludes questions involving impulsive tensions in strings.

Two particles \(P\) and \(Q\), masses \(8kg\) and \(2kg\), are connected by a light inextensible slack string. \(P\) is projected away from \(Q\) at \(4m\,s^{-1}\). After the string becomes taut, both move with common speed \(v\):

\[
8(4)+2(0)=8v+2v
\]

\[
32=10v
\]

\[
v=3.2.
\]

Impulse on \(Q\):

\[
I=2(3.2-0)=6.4Ns.
\]

### 8.19 Example: algebraic masses and speeds

Particles \(A\) and \(B\), masses \(m\) and \(4m\), move in the same direction with speeds \(5u\) and \(u\). After collision, \(B\) moves at \(2u\). Let \(A\)'s final velocity be \(v\):

\[
m(5u)+4m(u)=mv+4m(2u)
\]

\[
5mu+4mu=mv+8mu
\]

\[
9mu=mv+8mu
\]

\[
mu=mv
\]

\[
v=u.
\]

Impulse exerted by \(A\) on \(B\):

\[
I=4m(2u-u)=4mu.
\]

### 8.20 Coalescing particles

**Boundary warning:** Coalescing particles are ordinary A22/cross-board bridge material, not central FA22 Restitution.

If particles coalesce, they join together and move with a common velocity.

Example: \(P\) has mass \(150g=0.15kg\), at rest. \(Q\) has mass \(100g=0.1kg\), speed \(u\). They join and move at \(4m\,s^{-1}\). Combined mass \(0.25kg\):

\[
0.15(0)+0.1u=0.25(4)
\]

\[
0.1u=1
\]

\[
u=10m\,s^{-1}.
\]

### 8.21 Explosion-style bridge example

**Boundary warning:** Explosions are ordinary A22/cross-board bridge enrichment only.

A \(150g\) charge moving at \(4m\,s^{-1}\) splits into \(100g\) and \(50g\). The larger part continues in the same direction at twice the speed of the smaller part. Let smaller speed be \(v\), so smaller velocity \(-v\), larger velocity \(2v\):

\[
0.15(4)=0.05(-v)+0.1(2v)
\]

\[
0.6=-0.05v+0.2v
\]

\[
0.6=0.15v
\]

\[
v=4.
\]

Smaller speed \(4m\,s^{-1}\), larger speed \(8m\,s^{-1}\).

### 8.22 Momentum and impulse as vectors

For vector velocities:

\[
\mathbf{I}=m(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}).
\]

If:

\[
\mathbf{u}=a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j},\qquad \mathbf{v}=c\mathbf{i}+d\mathbf{j},
\]

then:

\[
\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}=(c-a)\mathbf{i}+(d-b)\mathbf{j}.
\]

### 8.23 Vector impulse: new velocity after an impulse

A particle of mass \(0.2kg\) has velocity \((10\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j})m\,s^{-1}\) and receives impulse \((3\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j})Ns\). Find new velocity \(\mathbf{v}\):

\[
3\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}=0.2(\mathbf{v}-(10\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j})).
\]

Divide by \(0.2\):

\[
15\mathbf{i}-10\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{v}-(10\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}).
\]

Add \(10\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}\):

\[
\mathbf{v}=25\mathbf{i}-15\mathbf{j}.
\]

### 8.24 Vector impulse: magnitude and direction

A puck of mass \(0.17kg\) changes velocity from \((10\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j})\) to \((15\mathbf{i}-7\mathbf{j})\). Its impulse \(\mathbf{Q}\) is:

\[
\mathbf{Q}=0.17((15\mathbf{i}-7\mathbf{j})-(10\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j}))
\]

\[
=0.17(5\mathbf{i}-12\mathbf{j})
\]

\[
=0.85\mathbf{i}-2.04\mathbf{j}.
\]

Magnitude:

\[
|\mathbf{Q}|=\sqrt{0.85^2+(-2.04)^2}=\sqrt{4.8841}=2.21Ns\quad(3\text{ s.f.}).
\]

Angle with positive \(\mathbf{i}\):

\[
\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2.04}{0.85}\right)=67.4^\circ.
\]

Since the \(\mathbf{j}\)-component is negative, this is \(67.4^\circ\) below the positive \(\mathbf{i}\)-direction.

### 8.25 How this foundation grows into FA22 Restitution

This lesson gives the collision equation:

\[
m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2.
\]

This is one equation. When two final velocities are unknown, one equation is not enough. FA22 Restitution supplies an additional relationship involving relative speeds. That law is not supplied in the current evidence, so it is not taught here.

## 11. Worked Examples

### Worked Example 1: calculating momentum with unit conversion

A ball of mass \(300g\) travels at \(2m\,s^{-1}\). Find momentum.

\[
300g=0.3kg
\]

\[
P=mv=0.3\times2=0.6Ns.
\]

\[
\boxed{0.6Ns}
\]

### Worked Example 2: braking force and time

A \(600kg\) car travels at \(9m\,s^{-1}\). A \(900N\) braking force resists motion. Find time to rest.

Choose motion as positive:

\[
F=-900,\quad m=600,\quad u=9,\quad v=0.
\]

\[
-900t=600(0-9)=-5400
\]

\[
t=6s.
\]

### Worked Example 3: resistive force from soil

A \(2kg\) ball moving downward at \(15m\,s^{-1}\) stops in \(0.4s\). Downward positive, soil resistance \(-R\):

\[
(-R)(0.4)=2(0-15)
\]

\[
-0.4R=-30
\]

\[
R=75N.
\]

### Worked Example 4: magnitude of impulse from the floor

A \(3kg\) stone falls at \(18m\,s^{-1}\) and is brought to rest. Upward positive:

\[
u=-18,\quad v=0.
\]

\[
I=3(0-(-18))=54Ns.
\]

### Worked Example 5: bullet brought to rest in wood

A \(30g=0.03kg\) bullet moving at \(400m\,s^{-1}\) stops in \(0.02s\). Motion positive, resistance \(-R\):

\[
(-R)(0.02)=0.03(0-400)=-12
\]

\[
R=600N.
\]

### Worked Example 6: truck rebounding from buffers

A \(1400kg\) truck moving at \(2m\,s^{-1}\) rebounds at \(1m\,s^{-1}\). Buffer force \(3500N\). Take buffer-force direction positive:

\[
3500t=1400(1-(-2))=4200
\]

\[
t=1.2s.
\]

### Worked Example 7: wall impulse and Newton’s third law

A \(0.2kg\) ball hits a wall at \(3.5m\,s^{-1}\) and rebounds at \(2.5m\,s^{-1}\). Take away from the wall positive:

\[
u=-3.5,\quad v=2.5.
\]

\[
I=0.2(2.5-(-3.5))=0.2(6)=1.2Ns.
\]

The impulse on the wall by the ball has the same magnitude:

\[
\boxed{1.2Ns}.
\]

### Worked Example 8: direct collision using PCLM

\[
2(3)+3(0)=2v+3\left(\frac73\right)
\]

\[
6=2v+7
\]

\[
v=-\frac12.
\]

So \(P\) has speed \(0.5m\,s^{-1}\) and direction reversed.

Impulse magnitude:

\[
I=2(0.5-(-3))=7Ns.
\]

### Worked Example 9: particles moving towards each other

\[
2(3)+4(-2)=2(-2)+4v
\]

\[
6-8=-4+4v
\]

\[
v=0.5.
\]

Impulse on \(B\):

\[
I=4(0.5-(-2))=10Ns.
\]

### Worked Example 10: common speed after string goes taut

**Off-spec for FA22 core; enrichment only.**

\[
8(4)+2(0)=8v+2v
\]

\[
32=10v
\]

\[
v=3.2m\,s^{-1}.
\]

Impulse on \(Q\):

\[
I=2(3.2-0)=6.4Ns.
\]

### Worked Example 11: given impulse, find velocities

Given impulse magnitude \(7Ns\), masses \(2kg\) and \(4kg\), initial velocities \(3\) and \(-2\).

For \(A\), take impulse direction positive and let final velocity drawn original-positive be \(x\):

\[
7=2((-x)-(-3))=2(-x+3)
\]

\[
7=-2x+6
\]

\[
x=-0.5.
\]

For \(B\):

\[
7=4(y-(-2))=4(y+2)
\]

\[
7=4y+8
\]

\[
y=-0.25.
\]

### Worked Example 12: algebraic masses and speeds

\[
m(5u)+4m(u)=mv+4m(2u)
\]

\[
9mu=mv+8mu
\]

\[
v=u.
\]

Impulse on \(B\):

\[
I=4m(2u-u)=4mu.
\]

### Worked Example 13: coalescing particles

**Bridge/enrichment only.**

\[
150g=0.15kg,\quad 100g=0.1kg.
\]

\[
0.15(0)+0.1u=0.25(4)
\]

\[
u=10m\,s^{-1}.
\]

### Worked Example 14: explosion-style conservation

**Bridge/enrichment only.**

\[
0.15(4)=0.05(-v)+0.1(2v)
\]

\[
0.6=0.15v
\]

\[
v=4.
\]

Smaller part speed \(4m\,s^{-1}\), larger part speed \(8m\,s^{-1}\).

### Worked Example 15: vector impulse, finding new velocity

\[
3\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}=0.2(\mathbf{v}-(10\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}))
\]

\[
15\mathbf{i}-10\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{v}-(10\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j})
\]

\[
\mathbf{v}=25\mathbf{i}-15\mathbf{j}.
\]

### Worked Example 16: vector impulse magnitude and angle

\[
\mathbf{Q}=0.17((15\mathbf{i}-7\mathbf{j})-(10\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j}))
\]

\[
=0.17(5\mathbf{i}-12\mathbf{j})=0.85\mathbf{i}-2.04\mathbf{j}.
\]

\[
|\mathbf{Q}|=\sqrt{0.85^2+(-2.04)^2}=2.21Ns.
\]

\[
\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2.04}{0.85}\right)=67.4^\circ.
\]

### Worked Example 17: vector impulse with unknown components

A \(5kg\) particle changes from velocity \(4\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}\) to \(B\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j}\) under impulse \(12\mathbf{i}+A\mathbf{j}\):

\[
12\mathbf{i}+A\mathbf{j}=5((B\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j})-(4\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}))
\]

\[
=5((B-4)\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j})=(5B-20)\mathbf{i}+15\mathbf{j}.
\]

Compare components:

\[
A=15,
\]

\[
12=5B-20,
\]

\[
B=\frac{32}{5}=6.4.
\]

## 12. Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

1. Treating speed and velocity as the same thing. If a ball rebounds, the final velocity has the opposite sign.
2. Forgetting to convert grams to kilograms.
3. Forgetting to convert \(km/h\) to \(m\,s^{-1}\).
4. Using \(m(u-v)\) instead of \(m(v-u)\).
5. Giving a negative value when magnitude was requested.
6. Writing a negative time; time is scalar, so check signs.
7. Using \(I=Ft\) when \(I=m(v-u)\) is simpler.
8. Confusing impulse on one particle with impulse on the other. They are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
9. Drawing velocity arrows as if they are force arrows.
10. Using PCLM when the system is not isolated.
11. Forgetting that “respectively” means “in that order”.
12. Treating “at rest” as an unknown instead of zero.
13. Not answering direction in words after a negative velocity.
14. Rounding exact fractions too early.
15. Importing impulsive tension in strings into FA22 core even though it is excluded.
16. Thinking PCLM is the whole FA22 Restitution method.

## 13. Practice Questions

All questions in this section are generated practice questions. They are not past-paper or textbook questions.

1. A particle of mass \(4kg\) moves with velocity \(7m\,s^{-1}\). Find its momentum.
2. A ball of mass \(250g\) moves with velocity \(12m\,s^{-1}\). Find its momentum in standard units.
3. A particle of mass \(3kg\) changes velocity from \(8m\,s^{-1}\) to \(2m\,s^{-1}\) in the same direction. Find the impulse, taking the original direction as positive.
4. A particle of mass \(3kg\) changes velocity from \(8m\,s^{-1}\) to \(2m\,s^{-1}\) in the opposite direction. Find the magnitude of the impulse.
5. A constant force of \(80N\) acts for \(0.25s\). Find impulse.
6. A \(20kg\) trolley moving at \(5m\,s^{-1}\) is stopped by a constant braking force of \(50N\). Find the time taken.
7. A \(0.5kg\) ball hits a wall at \(6m\,s^{-1}\) and rebounds at \(4m\,s^{-1}\). Find impulse magnitude.
8. A \(2kg\) particle has velocity \((3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j})m\,s^{-1}\). Find momentum vector and speed.
9. \(A\), mass \(2kg\), moves at \(6m\,s^{-1}\) and hits \(B\), mass \(4kg\), at rest. After collision, \(B\) moves at \(2m\,s^{-1}\). Find \(A\)'s final velocity.
10. \(A\), mass \(3kg\), and \(B\), mass \(5kg\), move towards each other with speeds \(4\) and \(2\). After collision, \(A\) rebounds at \(1\). Find \(B\)'s final velocity, taking \(A\)'s original direction positive.
11. \(P\), mass \(m\), and \(Q\), mass \(3m\), move in the same direction with speeds \(5u\) and \(2u\). After collision, \(Q\) moves at \(3u\). Find \(P\)'s final velocity.
12. In Question 9, find the magnitude of impulse on \(B\).
13. In Question 10, find the magnitude of impulse on \(A\).
14. A \(0.4kg\) particle with velocity \((5\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j})\) receives impulse \((2\mathbf{i}+6\mathbf{j})Ns\). Find new velocity.

16. \(A\), mass \(2kg\), moves right at \(5\). \(B\), mass \(1kg\), moves right at \(2\). After collision, \(A\) moves right at \(3\). Find \(B\)'s velocity, impulse magnitude on \(A\), and explain why PCLM alone works here.
17. A \(2kg\) particle with velocity \((6\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j})\) receives impulse \((-8\mathbf{i}+12\mathbf{j})Ns\). Find new velocity, speed after impulse, and one common mistake.

## 14. Worked Solutions

1. \(P=4\times7=28Ns\).

2. \(250g=0.25kg\), so \(P=0.25\times12=3Ns\).

3. \(I=3(2-8)=3(-6)=-18Ns\). The impulse acts opposite to the original direction.

4. Rebound means \(v=-2\), so \(I=3(-2-8)=-30Ns\). Magnitude \(30Ns\).

5. \(I=Ft=80\times0.25=20Ns\).

6. Choose motion positive. \(F=-50\), \(m=20\), \(u=5\), \(v=0\):

\[
-50t=20(0-5)=-100,
\]

so \(t=2s\).

7. Take away from wall positive. \(u=-6\), \(v=4\), \(m=0.5\):

\[
I=0.5(4-(-6))=5Ns.
\]

8. \(\mathbf{P}=2(3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j})=6\mathbf{i}+8\mathbf{j}\). Speed \(=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5m\,s^{-1}\).

9. \[
2(6)+4(0)=2v+4(2)
\]
\[
12=2v+8
\]
\[
v=2m\,s^{-1}.
\]

10. Take \(A\)'s original direction positive:

\[
3(4)+5(-2)=3(-1)+5v
\]

\[
12-10=-3+5v
\]

\[
2=-3+5v
\]

\[
v=1m\,s^{-1}.
\]

11. \[
m(5u)+3m(2u)=mv+3m(3u)
\]

\[
5mu+6mu=mv+9mu
\]

\[
2mu=mv
\]

\[
v=2u.
\]

12. For \(B\), \(m=4\), \(u=0\), \(v=2\):

\[
I=4(2-0)=8Ns.
\]

13. For \(A\), \(m=3\), \(u=4\), \(v=-1\):

\[
I=3(-1-4)=-15Ns,
\]

magnitude \(15Ns\).

14. \[
2\mathbf{i}+6\mathbf{j}=0.4(\mathbf{v}-(5\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}))
\]

Divide by \(0.4\):

\[
5\mathbf{i}+15\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{v}-(5\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j})
\]

\[
\mathbf{v}=10\mathbf{i}+13\mathbf{j}.
\]

15. \[
\mathbf{I}=0.5((-2\mathbf{i}+7\mathbf{j})-(4\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}))
\]

\[
=0.5(-6\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j})=-3\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}.
\]

\[
|\mathbf{I}|=\sqrt{(-3)^2+2^2}=\sqrt{13}Ns.
\]

16. Right positive:

\[
2(5)+1(2)=2(3)+v
\]

\[
12=6+v
\]

\[
v=6m\,s^{-1}.
\]

Impulse on \(A\):

\[
I=2(3-5)=-4Ns,
\]

magnitude \(4Ns\). PCLM works because only one final velocity is unknown. Full restitution problems with two unknown final velocities need another equation.

17. \[
-8\mathbf{i}+12\mathbf{j}=2(\mathbf{v}-(6\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}))
\]

Divide by \(2\):

\[
-4\mathbf{i}+6\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{v}-(6\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j})
\]

\[
\mathbf{v}=2\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}.
\]

Speed:

\[
|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{2^2+4^2}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5m\,s^{-1}.
\]

Common mistake: using \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\) instead of \(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}\).

## 15. Exam Technique Notes

Start by identifying the event, then define \(u\) and \(v\) for that event. Draw a before/after diagram. Choose and declare a positive direction. Use detached arrows for velocities and attached arrows for forces/impulses. Use exact values and preserve fractions until the final answer. Interpret negative answers correctly. Magnitude answers must be positive.

| Situation | Formula |
|---|---|
| momentum | \(P=mv\) |
| impulse from velocity change | \(I=m(v-u)\) |
| impulse from constant force and time | \(I=Ft\) |
| force-time and velocity change | \(Ft=m(v-u)\) |
| isolated collision | \(m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2\) |
| vector impulse | \(\mathbf{I}=m(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u})\) |

For a full direct elastic collision in FA22 Restitution, you need conservation of linear momentum and Newton’s law of restitution. This lesson supplies the first engine only.
