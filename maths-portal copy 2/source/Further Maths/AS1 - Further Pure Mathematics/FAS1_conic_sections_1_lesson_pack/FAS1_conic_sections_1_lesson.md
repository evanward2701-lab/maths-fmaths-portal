# 4. Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. explain what a conic section is, using the idea of a plane intersecting a non-solid double cone;
2. recognise the four main conic families: circle, ellipse, parabola and hyperbola;

7. convert \(x=ct,\ y=\dfrac ct\) into \(xy=c^2\);
8. use the standard horizontal parabola \(y^2=4ax,\ a>0\);
9. use the parametric point \(P(at^2,2at)\);
10. state the focus, directrix, vertex and axis of symmetry of \(y^2=4ax\);
11. prove \(y^2=4ax\) from the focus-directrix locus definition;
12. solve coordinate-geometry problems involving parabolas;
13. use the rectangular hyperbola \(xy=c^2,\ c>0\);
14. use the parametric point \(P\left(ct,\dfrac ct\right),\ t\ne0\);
15. derive tangent and normal equations using parametric differentiation;
16. solve longer conic problems involving intersections, midpoints, areas and loci.

## Bridge Objectives

You should be able to connect this lesson to ordinary A-Level Maths by recognising that:

- \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) is the familiar vertical parabola;
- \(y^2=4ax\) is a horizontal parabola, not generally a function \(y=f(x)\);
- \(y=k/x\) is the familiar reciprocal graph, now renamed as a rectangular hyperbola;
- coordinate geometry skills still work: gradient, line equations, simultaneous equations, midpoint and distance;
- differentiation still gives gradients of tangents;
- the normal gradient is still the negative reciprocal of the tangent gradient;
- parametric equations can be more efficient than Cartesian equations when points are given as \((at^2,2at)\) or \((ct,c/t)\).

You should be able to:

In Further Maths, the same idea becomes a more structured study of curves that come from cone sections. # 6. Big Picture Explanation

A **conic section** is a curve obtained when a plane intersects a non-solid double cone. The evidence describes four main families of conics:

- a **circle**, obtained when the plane is parallel to the base of the cone;
- an **ellipse**, obtained when the plane is less steep than the surface of the cone;
- a **parabola**, obtained when the plane is parallel to the surface of the cone;
- a **hyperbola**, obtained when the plane is steeper than the surface of the cone, so it intersects both cones.

A crucial warning from the evidence:

> Conics are lines. The shaded area is not included.

So the conic is the boundary curve, not the filled region on the cone diagram.

This first conics lesson focuses on two curves:

\[
\boxed{y^2=4ax}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{xy=c^2}.
\]

The first is a **horizontal parabola**. The second is a **rectangular hyperbola**, the formal conic name for the reciprocal-style curve you may already know.

The evidence explains that these curves are not just abstract decorations. For example, a hanging cable of a suspension bridge is hyperbolic if hanging freely and parabolic if attached by supporting cables to the road below.

The big mathematical story is this:

1. Ordinary Maths gave you curves.
2. Further Maths gives those curves geometry.
3. The geometry gives you better equations.
4. The equations give you efficient coordinate methods.
5. The parameters let you pick points without solving square roots every two minutes.

Keep the symbol roles contained:

- \(x,y\) are variables or coordinates;
- \(t,p,q\) are parameters;
- \(a,c,k\) are constants;
- \(P,Q,R,S,M,A,B,F\) are points;
- \(C,H,l,n,t\) may be curves or lines depending on context, so define them before using them.

# 7. Key Definitions and Notation

## 7.1 Cartesian Equation

A **Cartesian equation** says directly how the coordinate variables are related.

Examples:

\[
x+2y=1,
\]

\[
2x-3y^2=2.
\]

In a Cartesian equation, there is no hidden parameter. The equation describes all points \((x,y)\) satisfying the relation.

## 7.2 Parametric Equation

A **parametric equation** expresses each coordinate in terms of an independent variable called a **parameter**.

Example:

\[
x=\tan^2\theta,\qquad y=\sin\theta.
\]

Here, \(\theta\) is the parameter. Choosing a value of \(\theta\) gives one point \((x,y)\) on the curve.

For this lesson, the two main parametric forms are:

\[
x=at^2,\qquad y=2at
\]

and

\[
x=ct,\qquad y=\frac ct.
\]

## 7.3 Variables, Parameters and Constants

| Symbol type | Examples | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Variables | \(x,y\) | Coordinates of a general point, or variables in the curve equation. |
| Parameters | \(t,p,q,\theta\) | Values that vary to select a particular point on a curve. |
| Constants | \(a,c,k,l\) | Fixed numbers that determine the shape or size of the curve or situation. |
| Points | \(P,Q,R,S,M,A,B,F\) | Named coordinates in a problem. |
| Curves/lines | \(C,H,l,n\) | A named curve, hyperbola, line or normal, depending on the question. |

The parameter is “happening in the background” of the parametric form, while constants such as \(a\) and \(c\) determine the specific conic being studied.

## 7.4 Locus

A **locus of points** is a set of points satisfying a certain condition.

| Condition | Resulting locus |
|---|---|
| A fixed distance from a point | Circle |
| A fixed distance from a line | A pair of parallel lines, or a capsule shape if using a line segment |
| Equidistant from two points | Perpendicular bisector |
| Equidistant from two lines | Angle bisector |
| Equidistant from a point and a line | Parabola |

## 7.5 Focus and Directrix

For a parabola:

- the fixed point is called the **focus**;
- the fixed line is called the **directrix**.

A parabola is the locus of points equidistant from the focus and the directrix.

## 7.6 Standard Horizontal Parabola

A horizontally aligned parabola centred at the origin has Cartesian equation:

\[
\boxed{y^2=4ax},\qquad a>0.
\]

It has parametric equations:

\[
\boxed{x=at^2,\qquad y=2at,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.}
\]

A general point is:

\[
P(x,y)=P(at^2,2at).
\]

For \(y^2=4ax\), the key features are:

\[
\text{Focus }S=(a,0),
\]

\[
\text{Directrix: }x=-a,
\]

\[
\text{Vertex }V=(0,0),
\]

\[
\text{Axis of symmetry: }y=0.
\]

## 7.7 Standard Rectangular Hyperbola

A rectangular hyperbola with asymptotes \(x=0,\ y=0\) has Cartesian equation:

\[
\boxed{xy=c^2}
\]

or equivalently

\[
\boxed{y=\frac{c^2}{x}},
\]

where \(c>0\).

It has parametric equations:

\[
\boxed{x=ct,\qquad y=\frac ct,\qquad t\in\mathbb R,\ t\ne0.}
\]

A general point is:

\[
P(x,y)=P\left(ct,\frac ct\right).
\]

The evidence explicitly says that at this stage you do **not yet** need to know how to find the vertices, foci or directrices of this rectangular hyperbola.

## 7.8 Tangent and Normal

At a point on a curve:

- the **tangent** is the line touching the curve with the same gradient as the curve at that point;
- the **normal** is perpendicular to the tangent.

If the tangent gradient is \(m\), then the normal gradient is:

\[
-\frac1m,
\]

provided \(m\ne0\).

For parametric curves:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{\frac{dy}{dt}}{\frac{dx}{dt}},
\]

as long as \(\frac{dx}{dt}\ne0\).

# 8. Core Theory

## 8.1 The Conic Families

A conic section comes from slicing a non-solid double cone with a plane.

| Conic | How it is obtained from the cone | Familiarity in the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Circle | Plane is parallel to the base of the cones. | First seen at GCSE/Pure Year 1. |
| Ellipse | Plane is less steep than the surface of the cone. | First seen in Chapter 3. |
| Parabola | Plane is parallel to the surface of the cone. | First seen at GCSE/Pure Year 1. |
| Hyperbola | Plane is steeper than the cone surface, so it intersects both cones. | First seen in Chapter 3; rectangular hyperbola appears in this chapter. |

A hyperbola has two branches because the cutting plane intersects both cones.

## 8.2 Cartesian vs Parametric Equations

### Cartesian Form

A Cartesian equation relates \(x\) and \(y\) directly.

Example:

\[
x+2y=1.
\]

This can be rearranged to:

\[
2y=1-x,
\]

\[
y=\frac{1-x}{2}.
\]

### Parametric Form

A parametric equation expresses \(x\) and \(y\) in terms of a third quantity, the parameter.

Example:

\[
x=\tan^2\theta,\qquad y=\sin\theta.
\]

If \(\theta=30^\circ\), then:

\[
x=\tan^2 30^\circ.
\]

Since

\[
\tan 30^\circ=\frac1{\sqrt3},
\]

we get:

\[
x=\left(\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)^2=\frac13.
\]

Also:

\[
y=\sin30^\circ=\frac12.
\]

So the point generated is:

\[
\left(\frac13,\frac12\right).
\]

The curve is produced by allowing the parameter to vary.

## 8.3 Ladder Midpoint Example

A ladder of length \(2l\) is initially vertical against a wall. It gradually slides down to the floor. We consider the trajectory of its midpoint \((x,y)\).

The diagram shows the midpoint halfway along the ladder, so the distance from the corner to the midpoint is \(l\). This gives a right-angled triangle.

By Pythagoras:

\[
x^2+y^2=l^2.
\]

So the Cartesian equation of the trajectory is:

\[
\boxed{x^2+y^2=l^2.}
\]

This is a circle of radius \(l\).

A parameter \(\theta\) may be introduced as the angular inclination of the ladder. The corresponding scaled form is:

\[
x=l\cos\theta,\qquad y=l\sin\theta
\]

or, depending on which axis the angle is measured from,

\[
x=l\sin\theta,\qquad y=l\cos\theta.
\]

**Bridge Note:** In ordinary A-Level Maths, you may have met circle equations and trigonometry separately. Here, the same curve can be described either by a direct Cartesian relation or by a parameter.

## 8.4 Converting Parametric Equations to Cartesian Equations

The evidence says we can use either **substitution** or **elimination** to turn parametric equations into a Cartesian equation.

### 8.4.1 Parabola Conversion

Given:

\[
x=at^2,\qquad y=2at,
\]

where \(a>0\) is a constant and \(t\in\mathbb R\).

We want to eliminate \(t\).

Start with:

\[
y=2at.
\]

Make \(t\) the subject:

\[
t=\frac{y}{2a}.
\]

Substitute into:

\[
x=at^2.
\]

So:

\[
x
=
a\left(\frac{y}{2a}\right)^2.
\]

Square the fraction carefully:

\[
\left(\frac{y}{2a}\right)^2
=
\frac{y^2}{(2a)^2}
=
\frac{y^2}{4a^2}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
x
=
a\cdot \frac{y^2}{4a^2}.
\]

Cancel one factor of \(a\):

\[
x=\frac{y^2}{4a}.
\]

Multiply both sides by \(4a\):

\[
4ax=y^2.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{y^2=4ax.}
\]

This proves that \(x=at^2,\ y=2at\) is a parametric form of the parabola \(y^2=4ax\).

**Common warning:** When squaring \(2a\),

\[
(2a)^2=4a^2,
\]

not \(2a^2\).

### 8.4.2 Rectangular Hyperbola Conversion

Given:

\[
x=ct,\qquad y=\frac ct,
\]

where \(c>0\) is a constant and \(t\in\mathbb R,\ t\ne0\).

Method 1: substitution.

From \(x=ct\), make \(t\) the subject:

\[
t=\frac{x}{c}.
\]

Substitute into \(y=\frac ct\):

\[
y=
\frac{c}{x/c}.
\]

Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal:

\[
y=c\cdot\frac{c}{x}.
\]

Hence:

\[
y=\frac{c^2}{x}.
\]

Multiply by \(x\):

\[
xy=c^2.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{xy=c^2.}
\]

Method 2: direct multiplication.

Since

\[
x=ct,\qquad y=\frac ct,
\]

multiply:

\[
xy=(ct)\left(\frac ct\right).
\]

Cancel \(t\), remembering \(t\ne0\):

\[
xy=c^2.
\]

So again:

\[
\boxed{xy=c^2.}
\]

**Bridge Note:** In ordinary Maths, \(y=\frac{k}{x}\) was a reciprocal graph. Here, \(xy=c^2\) is the same shape family, but its formal conic name is a rectangular hyperbola.

## 8.5 Sketching the Parabola from Its Parametric Form

For

\[
x=at^2,\qquad y=2at,\qquad a>0,
\]

choose several values of \(t\).

| \(t\) | \(x=at^2\) | \(y=2at\) | Point |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| \(-2\) | \(4a\) | \(-4a\) | \((4a,-4a)\) |
| \(-1\) | \(a\) | \(-2a\) | \((a,-2a)\) |
| \(0\) | \(0\) | \(0\) | \((0,0)\) |
| \(1\) | \(a\) | \(2a\) | \((a,2a)\) |
| \(2\) | \(4a\) | \(4a\) | \((4a,4a)\) |

Notice:

- \(x=at^2\ge0\), so the curve lies on or to the right of the \(y\)-axis;
- positive and negative \(t\) values give symmetric \(y\)-values;
- the curve passes through \((0,0)\);
- the curve opens to the right.

## 8.6 From Ordinary Parabolas to Horizontal Parabolas

In ordinary Maths, a vertical parabola often has the form:

\[
y=ax^2+bx+c.
\]

If centred at the origin, this simplifies to:

\[
y=ax^2.
\]

The line of symmetry is vertical.

In this chapter, the evidence focuses on horizontally aligned parabolas. The informal idea is to swap \(x\) and \(y\), giving an equation of the type:

\[
x=ay^2.
\]

The standard conic form is written as:

\[
\boxed{y^2=4ax}
\]

with \(a>0\).

The factor \(4a\) is chosen so that the focus and directrix have the clean forms:

\[
S=(a,0),
\]

\[
x=-a.
\]

**Bridge Note:** In ordinary Maths, the number multiplying \(x^2\) controlled the width of a vertical parabola. Here, \(a\) controls the width of the horizontal parabola and also fixes the focus/directrix positions.

## 8.7 The Standard Parabola \(y^2=4ax\)

For the standard horizontal parabola:

\[
\boxed{y^2=4ax,\qquad a>0,}
\]

we use the parametric form:

\[
\boxed{x=at^2,\qquad y=2at,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.}
\]

A general point is:

\[
P(at^2,2at).
\]

The key features are:

\[
\text{Focus }S=(a,0),
\]

\[
\text{Directrix: }x=-a,
\]

\[
\text{Vertex }V=(0,0),
\]

\[
\text{Axis of symmetry: }y=0.
\]

A parabola is a locus of points such that the distance from any point to the focus is the same as the distance to the directrix.

- the directrix labelled \(DIRECTRIX\);
- the vertex labelled \(VERTEX\);
- the axis of symmetry labelled \(AXIS OF SYMMETRY\);
- the focus at \((a,0)\);
- the directrix line \(x=-a\);
- the vertex at \((0,0)\).

## 8.8 Focus-Directrix Definition of a Parabola

A parabola is the locus of points equidistant from:

- a fixed point called the focus;
- a fixed line called the directrix.

For the standard parabola \(y^2=4ax\), the focus is \(S=(a,0)\), and the directrix is \(x=-a\).

Let \(P(x,y)\) be any point on the locus.

The distance from \(P(x,y)\) to the focus \(S(a,0)\) is:

\[
PS=\sqrt{(x-a)^2+(y-0)^2}.
\]

So:

\[
PS=\sqrt{(x-a)^2+y^2}.
\]

The perpendicular distance from \(P(x,y)\) to the directrix \(x=-a\) is:

\[
x-(-a)=x+a.
\]

For the standard right-opening parabola, \(x+a\ge0\), so the distance is \(x+a\).

The locus condition is:

\[
PS=\text{distance to directrix}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\sqrt{(x-a)^2+y^2}=x+a.
\]

Square both sides:

\[
(x-a)^2+y^2=(x+a)^2.
\]

Expand the left-hand side:

\[
(x-a)^2+y^2
=
x^2-2ax+a^2+y^2.
\]

Expand the right-hand side:

\[
(x+a)^2=x^2+2ax+a^2.
\]

So:

\[
x^2-2ax+a^2+y^2=x^2+2ax+a^2.
\]

Subtract \(x^2\) from both sides:

\[
-2ax+a^2+y^2=2ax+a^2.
\]

Subtract \(a^2\) from both sides:

\[
-2ax+y^2=2ax.
\]

Add \(2ax\) to both sides:

\[
y^2=4ax.
\]

So the locus is:

\[
\boxed{y^2=4ax.}
\]

## 8.9 Finding the Equation of a Parabola from Focus and Directrix

For the standard horizontal parabola:

\[
y^2=4ax,
\]

we know:

\[
\text{Focus }=(a,0),
\]

\[
\text{Directrix }x=-a.
\]

### Example 1

Find an equation of the parabola with focus \((7,0)\) and directrix \(x+7=0\).

First rewrite the directrix:

\[
x+7=0
\]

\[
x=-7.
\]

So:

\[
a=7.
\]

Use \(y^2=4ax\).

Substitute \(a=7\):

\[
y^2=4(7)x.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{y^2=28x.}
\]

A parametric form would be:

\[
x=7t^2,\qquad y=14t.
\]

### Example 2

Find an equation of the parabola with focus \(\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{4},0\right)\) and directrix \(x=-\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\).

So:

\[
a=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}.
\]

Use \(y^2=4ax\). Substitute:

\[
y^2=4\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\right)x.
\]

Cancel \(4\):

\[
y^2=\sqrt3 x.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{y^2=\sqrt3 x.}
\]

A parametric form would be:

\[
x=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}t^2,\qquad y=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}t.
\]

## 8.11 Coordinate Geometry with Parabolas

The evidence says this section is “nothing really new” in terms of technique: it is a recap of Pure Year 1 coordinate geometry, but set in a richer conic context.

### Worked Example: Line through the Focus and a Point on the Parabola

A point \(P(8,-8)\) lies on the parabola \(C\) with equation \(y^2=8x\). The point \(S\) is the focus of the parabola. The line \(l\) passes through \(S\) and \(P\).

#### Part (a): Find the coordinates of \(S\)

Compare \(y^2=8x\) with \(y^2=4ax\).

So:

\[
4a=8.
\]

Divide by \(4\):

\[
a=2.
\]

The focus is \(S=(a,0)\). Therefore:

\[
\boxed{S=(2,0).}
\]

#### Part (b): Find the equation of \(l\)

The line \(l\) passes through \(S(2,0)\) and \(P(8,-8)\).

Gradient:

\[
m=\frac{0-(-8)}{2-8}.
\]

Simplify:

\[
m=\frac{8}{-6}=-\frac43.
\]

Use point-gradient form with \(S(2,0)\):

\[
y-0=-\frac43(x-2).
\]

So:

\[
y=-\frac43(x-2).
\]

Multiply by \(3\):

\[
3y=-4(x-2).
\]

Expand:

\[
3y=-4x+8.
\]

Bring all terms to one side:

\[
4x+3y-8=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{l:\ 4x+3y-8=0.}
\]

#### Part (c): Find the second intersection point \(Q\)

Solve simultaneously:

\[
C:\quad y^2=8x
\]

and

\[
l:\quad 4x+3y-8=0.
\]

From the parabola:

\[
x=\frac{y^2}{8}.
\]

Substitute into the line equation:

\[
4\left(\frac{y^2}{8}\right)+3y-8=0.
\]

Simplify:

\[
\frac12y^2+3y-8=0.
\]

Multiply by \(2\):

\[
y^2+6y-16=0.
\]

Factorise:

\[
y^2+6y-16=(y+8)(y-2).
\]

So:

\[
(y+8)(y-2)=0.
\]

Hence \(y=-8\) or \(y=2\).

The value \(y=-8\) gives the original point \(P(8,-8)\). The second point \(Q\) has \(y=2\).

Substitute into \(x=\frac{y^2}{8}\):

\[
x=\frac{2^2}{8}=\frac4{8}=\frac12.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{Q=\left(\frac12,2\right).}
\]

#### Part (d): Find the midpoint \(M\) of \(P\) and \(Q\)

We have:

\[
P=(8,-8),
\qquad
Q=\left(\frac12,2\right).
\]

The midpoint is:

\[
M=\left(\frac{8+\frac12}{2},\frac{-8+2}{2}\right).
\]

For the \(x\)-coordinate:

\[
8+\frac12=\frac{16}{2}+\frac12=\frac{17}{2}.
\]

Then:

\[
\frac{\frac{17}{2}}{2}=\frac{17}{4}.
\]

For the \(y\)-coordinate:

\[
-8+2=-6,
\]

so:

\[
\frac{-6}{2}=-3.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{M=\left(\frac{17}{4},-3\right).}
\]

#### Part (e): Sketch description

The sketch must show:

- the right-opening parabola \(C:y^2=8x\);
- the focus \(S(2,0)\);
- the point \(P(8,-8)\) on the lower branch;
- the point \(Q\left(\frac12,2\right)\) on the upper branch;
- the line \(l:4x+3y-8=0\) passing through \(P\), \(S\) and \(Q\);
- the midpoint \(M\left(\frac{17}{4},-3\right)\) between \(P\) and \(Q\).

## 8.12 Parametric Alternative for the Same Intersection

For \(y^2=8x\), we have \(4a=8\), so \(a=2\).

Therefore the parametric equations are:

\[
x=2t^2,\qquad y=4t.
\]

The line is:

\[
4x+3y-8=0.
\]

Substitute \(x=2t^2,\ y=4t\):

\[
4(2t^2)+3(4t)-8=0.
\]

Simplify:

\[
8t^2+12t-8=0.
\]

Divide by \(4\):

\[
2t^2+3t-2=0.
\]

Factorise:

\[
2t^2+3t-2=(2t-1)(t+2).
\]

Thus:

\[
(2t-1)(t+2)=0.
\]

So:

\[
t=\frac12
\]

or

\[
t=-2.
\]

For \(t=-2\):

\[
x=2(-2)^2=8,\qquad y=4(-2)=-8.
\]

This gives \(P=(8,-8)\).

For \(t=\frac12\):

\[
x=2\left(\frac12\right)^2=\frac12,\qquad y=4\left(\frac12\right)=2.
\]

This gives \(Q=\left(\frac12,2\right)\).

Same answer, different route.

**Exam technique:** Parametric form is often cleaner when the curve already has a natural parameter. Cartesian form is often cleaner when the question gives \(x\)- or \(y\)-coordinates directly.

## 8.13 Chord Cut by a Vertical Line

The parabola \(C\) has general point:

\[
(at^2,2at).
\]

The line \(x=k\) intersects \(C\) at the points \(P\) and \(Q\). Find, in terms of \(a\) and \(k\), the length of the chord \(PQ\).

Since \(x=at^2\) and the line is \(x=k\), set:

\[
k=at^2.
\]

Divide by \(a\):

\[
\frac{k}{a}=t^2.
\]

So:

\[
t=\pm\sqrt{\frac{k}{a}}.
\]

Now \(y=2at\).

For \(t=+\sqrt{\frac{k}{a}}\):

\[
y=2a\sqrt{\frac{k}{a}}.
\]

Rewrite:

\[
2a\sqrt{\frac{k}{a}}
=
2a\cdot\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{a}}.
\]

Since \(\frac{a}{\sqrt a}=\sqrt a\), we have:

\[
y=2\sqrt a\sqrt k=2\sqrt{ak}.
\]

For \(t=-\sqrt{\frac{k}{a}}\), we get:

\[
y=-2\sqrt{ak}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
P=(k,2\sqrt{ak}),\qquad Q=(k,-2\sqrt{ak}).
\]

The chord \(PQ\) is vertical, so its length is the difference between the \(y\)-coordinates:

\[
PQ=2\sqrt{ak}-(-2\sqrt{ak}).
\]

Therefore:

\[
PQ=4\sqrt{ak}.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{PQ=4\sqrt{ak}.}
\]

## 8.14 Rectangular Hyperbolas

A hyperbola is produced when the intersecting plane is steeper than the surface of the cone, so it intersects both cones. A hyperbola therefore has two branches. The evidence then narrows the chapter to a special case: the **rectangular hyperbola**.

A rectangular hyperbola is a hyperbola whose asymptotes are perpendicular. In this chapter, the only rectangular hyperbolas required are those whose asymptotes are the coordinate axes:

\[
x=0,\qquad y=0.
\]

The standard Cartesian equation is:

\[
\boxed{xy=c^2}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{y=\frac{c^2}{x}},
\]

where \(c>0\).

The parametric equations are:

\[
\boxed{x=ct,\qquad y=\frac ct,\qquad t\in\mathbb R,\ t\ne0.}
\]

A general point is:

\[
P\left(ct,\frac ct\right).
\]

The evidence says you do not yet need to know how to find the vertices, foci or directrices for this rectangular hyperbola.

### Reciprocal Graph Bridge

You have seen the graph \(y=\frac{k}{x}\) before. Up to this point you may have called it a reciprocal graph, but “reciprocal graph” is not the formal mathematical name for the line represented by this equation. Its formal conic name is a hyperbola, and in this specific axes-as-asymptotes case, a rectangular hyperbola.

## 8.15 Coordinate Geometry with Rectangular Hyperbolas

### Worked Example

The rectangular hyperbola has Cartesian equation:

\[
xy=64.
\]

The line:

\[
x+2y-36=0
\]

intersects the curve at points \(P\) and \(Q\).

Find the coordinates of \(P\) and \(Q\), and the equation of the perpendicular bisector of \(PQ\).

### Method 1: Parametric

For \(xy=64\), compare with \(xy=c^2\). So \(c^2=64\), and since \(c>0\), \(c=8\).

The parametric form is:

\[
x=8t,\qquad y=\frac8t.
\]

Substitute into the line:

\[
x+2y-36=0.
\]

So:

\[
8t+2\left(\frac8t\right)-36=0.
\]

Simplify:

\[
8t+\frac{16}{t}-36=0.
\]

Multiply by \(t\), remembering \(t\ne0\):

\[
8t^2+16-36t=0.
\]

Reorder:

\[
8t^2-36t+16=0.
\]

Divide by \(4\):

\[
2t^2-9t+4=0.
\]

Factorise:

\[
2t^2-9t+4=(2t-1)(t-4).
\]

So \(t=\frac12\) or \(t=4\).

For \(t=\frac12\):

\[
x=8\left(\frac12\right)=4,\qquad y=\frac8{1/2}=16.
\]

For \(t=4\):

\[
x=8(4)=32,\qquad y=\frac84=2.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{P=(4,16),\quad Q=(32,2)}
\]

or the other way round.

### Method 2: Cartesian

From the line:

\[
x+2y-36=0.
\]

Rearrange:

\[
2y=36-x,
\]

\[
y=\frac{36-x}{2}.
\]

Substitute into \(xy=64\):

\[
x\left(\frac{36-x}{2}\right)=64.
\]

Multiply by \(2\):

\[
x(36-x)=128.
\]

Expand:

\[
36x-x^2=128.
\]

Bring all terms to one side:

\[
x^2-36x+128=0.
\]

Factorise:

\[
x^2-36x+128=(x-4)(x-32).
\]

So \(x=4\) or \(x=32\). If \(x=4\), then \(y=16\). If \(x=32\), then \(y=2\). Again:

\[
\boxed{(4,16),\quad (32,2).}
\]

### Perpendicular Bisector of \(PQ\)

Let \(P=(4,16)\), \(Q=(32,2)\).

The midpoint is:

\[
M=\left(\frac{4+32}{2},\frac{16+2}{2}\right)=\left(18,9\right).
\]

The gradient of \(PQ\) is:

\[
m_{PQ}=\frac{2-16}{32-4}=\frac{-14}{28}=-\frac12.
\]

The gradient of the perpendicular bisector is the negative reciprocal:

\[
m_\perp=2.
\]

Use point-gradient form through \(M(18,9)\):

\[
y-9=2(x-18).
\]

Expand:

\[
y-9=2x-36.
\]

Add \(9\):

\[
y=2x-27.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{y=2x-27.}
\]

## 8.16 Tangents and Normals at Specific Points

### Rectangular Hyperbola Example

The point \(P\) where \(x=2\) lies on the rectangular hyperbola:

\[
xy=8.
\]

Find the equation of the tangent \(t\) and the equation of the normal \(n\).

Start with:

\[
xy=8.
\]

Make \(y\) the subject:

\[
y=\frac8x.
\]

Rewrite:

\[
y=8x^{-1}.
\]

Differentiate:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=8(-1)x^{-2}.
\]

So:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=-8x^{-2}.
\]

That is:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac8{x^2}.
\]

At \(x=2\):

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac8{2^2}=-\frac84=-2.
\]

So the tangent gradient is \(m_t=-2\).

Find \(y\) when \(x=2\):

\[
2y=8.
\]

Divide by \(2\):

\[
y=4.
\]

So:

\[
P=(2,4).
\]

### Tangent

Use \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\):

\[
y-4=-2(x-2).
\]

Expand:

\[
y-4=-2x+4.
\]

Bring all terms to one side:

\[
2x+y-8=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{2x+y-8=0.}
\]

### Normal

The normal gradient is the negative reciprocal of \(-2\):

\[
m_n=\frac12.
\]

Use:

\[
y-4=\frac12(x-2).
\]

Multiply by \(2\):

\[
2y-8=x-2.
\]

Bring all terms to one side:

\[
x-2y+6=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{x-2y+6=0.}
\]

## 8.17 Parametric Differentiation for the Parabola

For the parabola \(y^2=4ax\), use:

\[
x=at^2,\qquad y=2at.
\]

Differentiate both with respect to \(t\):

\[
\frac{dx}{dt}=2at,
\]

\[
\frac{dy}{dt}=2a.
\]

Then:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{\frac{dy}{dt}}{\frac{dx}{dt}}.
\]

Substitute:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{2a}{2at}.
\]

Cancel \(2a\):

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1t.
\]

So the tangent gradient at parameter \(t\) is:

\[
\boxed{\frac1t.}
\]

The normal gradient is the negative reciprocal:

\[
\boxed{-t.}
\]

## 8.18 Tangent to \(y^2=4ax\) at \(P(at^2,2at)\)

Point:

\[
P(at^2,2at).
\]

Gradient of tangent:

\[
m=\frac1t.
\]

Use point-gradient form:

\[
y-y_1=m(x-x_1).
\]

Substitute:

\[
y-2at=\frac1t(x-at^2).
\]

Multiply by \(t\):

\[
t(y-2at)=x-at^2.
\]

Expand:

\[
ty-2at^2=x-at^2.
\]

Add \(2at^2\) to both sides:

\[
ty=x-at^2+2at^2.
\]

Simplify:

\[
ty=x+at^2.
\]

So the tangent is:

\[
\boxed{ty=x+at^2.}
\]

## 8.19 Normal to \(y^2=4ax\) at \(P(at^2,2at)\)

Point:

\[
P(at^2,2at).
\]

Tangent gradient:

\[
\frac1t.
\]

Normal gradient:

\[
-t.
\]

Use:

\[
y-y_1=m(x-x_1).
\]

Substitute:

\[
y-2at=-t(x-at^2).
\]

Expand:

\[
y-2at=-tx+at^3.
\]

Add \(tx\) to both sides:

\[
y+tx-2at=at^3.
\]

Add \(2at\) to both sides:

\[
y+tx=2at+at^3.
\]

So the normal is:

\[
\boxed{y+tx=2at+at^3.}
\]

Equivalently:

\[
\boxed{y+tx=at(t^2+2).}
\]

## 8.20 Parametric Differentiation for the Rectangular Hyperbola

For the rectangular hyperbola \(xy=c^2\), use:

\[
x=ct,\qquad y=\frac ct.
\]

Rewrite:

\[
y=ct^{-1}.
\]

Differentiate:

\[
\frac{dx}{dt}=c.
\]

Also:

\[
\frac{dy}{dt}=c(-1)t^{-2}.
\]

So:

\[
\frac{dy}{dt}=-ct^{-2}=-\frac c{t^2}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{\frac{dy}{dt}}{\frac{dx}{dt}}
=
\frac{-c/t^2}{c}.
\]

Cancel \(c\):

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac1{t^2}.
\]

So the tangent gradient is:

\[
\boxed{-\frac1{t^2}.}
\]

The normal gradient is the negative reciprocal:

\[
\boxed{t^2.}
\]

## 8.21 Tangent to \(xy=c^2\) at \(P(ct,c/t)\)

Point:

\[
P\left(ct,\frac ct\right).
\]

Tangent gradient:

\[
-\frac1{t^2}.
\]

Use point-gradient form:

\[
y-y_1=m(x-x_1).
\]

Substitute:

\[
y-\frac ct=-\frac1{t^2}(x-ct).
\]

Multiply by \(t^2\):

\[
t^2y-ct=-(x-ct).
\]

Expand the right-hand side:

\[
t^2y-ct=-x+ct.
\]

Add \(x\) to both sides:

\[
x+t^2y-ct=ct.
\]

Add \(ct\) to both sides:

\[
x+t^2y=2ct.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{x+t^2y=2ct.}
\]

## 8.22 Normal to \(xy=c^2\) at \(P(cp,c/p)\)

The transcript uses \(p\) as the parameter for a general point on the rectangular hyperbola:

\[
P\left(cp,\frac cp\right).
\]

The tangent gradient is:

\[
-\frac1{p^2}.
\]

The normal gradient is:

\[
p^2.
\]

Use point-gradient form:

\[
y-\frac cp=p^2(x-cp).
\]

Multiply by \(p\) to remove the fraction:

\[
p\left(y-\frac cp\right)=p\cdot p^2(x-cp).
\]

Left-hand side:

\[
py-c.
\]

Right-hand side:

\[
p^3(x-cp).
\]

So:

\[
py-c=p^3(x-cp).
\]

Expand:

\[
py-c=p^3x-cp^4.
\]

Bring all terms to one side, with \(p^3x\) positive:

\[
0=p^3x-py+c-cp^4.
\]

Factor the final two terms:

\[
0=p^3x-py+c(1-p^4).
\]

Thus the normal is:

\[
\boxed{p^3x-py+c(1-p^4)=0.}
\]

## 8.23 Rectangular Hyperbola Normal Meets the Curve Again

Let:

\[
H:\ xy=c^2
\]

and let:

\[
P\left(cp,\frac cp\right)
\]

lie on \(H\).

The normal at \(P\) is:

\[
p^3x-py+c(1-p^4)=0.
\]

Suppose the normal meets \(H\) again at \(Q\).

From the hyperbola:

\[
xy=c^2.
\]

Make \(y\) the subject:

\[
y=\frac{c^2}{x}.
\]

Substitute into the normal:

\[
p^3x-p\left(\frac{c^2}{x}\right)+c(1-p^4)=0.
\]

So:

\[
p^3x-\frac{pc^2}{x}+c(1-p^4)=0.
\]

Multiply by \(x\):

\[
p^3x^2-pc^2+c(1-p^4)x=0.
\]

Reorder:

\[
p^3x^2+c(1-p^4)x-pc^2=0.
\]

This is a quadratic in \(x\). One solution is the known point \(P\), whose \(x\)-coordinate is:

\[
x=cp.
\]

Therefore \(x-cp\) is a factor.

We factor:

\[
p^3x^2+c(1-p^4)x-pc^2=(x-cp)(p^3x+c).
\]

Check by expanding:

\[
(x-cp)(p^3x+c)
=
p^3x^2+cx-cp\cdot p^3x-cp\cdot c.
\]

Simplify:

\[
=p^3x^2+cx-cp^4x-c^2p.
\]

Factor the \(x\)-terms:

\[
=p^3x^2+c(1-p^4)x-c^2p.
\]

Since \(-c^2p=-pc^2\), the factorisation is correct.

Set the second factor to zero:

\[
p^3x+c=0.
\]

Then:

\[
p^3x=-c.
\]

So:

\[
x=-\frac{c}{p^3}.
\]

Now find \(y\):

\[
y=\frac{c^2}{x}.
\]

Substitute:

\[
y=\frac{c^2}{-c/p^3}.
\]

Multiply by the reciprocal:

\[
y=c^2\left(-\frac{p^3}{c}\right).
\]

Cancel one \(c\):

\[
y=-cp^3.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{Q=\left(-\frac{c}{p^3},-cp^3\right).}
\]

The midpoint of \(P\) and \(Q\) is:

\[
M=\left(
\frac{cp-\frac{c}{p^3}}{2},
\frac{\frac cp-cp^3}{2}
\right).
\]

For the \(x\)-coordinate:

\[
\frac{cp-\frac c{p^3}}{2}
=
\frac12\left(cp-\frac c{p^3}\right).
\]

Use common denominator \(p^3\):

\[
cp=\frac{cp^4}{p^3}.
\]

So:

\[
cp-\frac c{p^3}
=
\frac{cp^4-c}{p^3}
=
\frac{c(p^4-1)}{p^3}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
x_M=\frac{c(p^4-1)}{2p^3}.
\]

For the \(y\)-coordinate:

\[
\frac{\frac cp-cp^3}{2}
=
\frac12\left(\frac cp-cp^3\right).
\]

Use common denominator \(p\):

\[
cp^3=\frac{cp^4}{p}.
\]

So:

\[
\frac cp-cp^3
=
\frac c p-\frac{cp^4}{p}
=
\frac{c(1-p^4)}{p}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
y_M=\frac{c(1-p^4)}{2p}.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
M=
\left(
\frac{c(p^4-1)}{2p^3},
\frac{c(1-p^4)}{2p}
\right).
}
\]

## 8.24 Problem Solving with a Parabola Normal and Area

Let the parabola \(C\) have equation:

\[
y^2=20x.
\]

A general point \(P\) on \(C\) is:

\[
P(5p^2,10p).
\]

The line \(l\) is normal to \(C\) at \(P\).

### Part (a): Show that an equation of the normal is

\[
\boxed{px+y=10p+5p^3.}
\]

Use the parametric point:

\[
x=5p^2,\qquad y=10p.
\]

Differentiate:

\[
\frac{dx}{dp}=10p,\qquad \frac{dy}{dp}=10.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{10}{10p}
=
\frac1p.
\]

This is the tangent gradient. So the normal gradient is:

\[
m_n=-p.
\]

The normal passes through \(P(5p^2,10p)\).

Use point-gradient form:

\[
y-10p=-p(x-5p^2).
\]

Expand:

\[
y-10p=-px+5p^3.
\]

Add \(px\) to both sides:

\[
px+y-10p=5p^3.
\]

Add \(10p\):

\[
px+y=10p+5p^3.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{px+y=10p+5p^3.}
\]

### Alternative implicit differentiation route

From \(y^2=20x\), differentiate implicitly:

\[
2y\frac{dy}{dx}=20.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{20}{2y}=\frac{10}{y}.
\]

At \(P(5p^2,10p)\):

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{10}{10p}=\frac1p.
\]

### Part (b): Use the fact that the normal passes through \((30,0)\)

The normal is:

\[
px+y=10p+5p^3.
\]

Substitute \(x=30,\ y=0\):

\[
30p=10p+5p^3.
\]

Subtract \(10p\):

\[
20p=5p^3.
\]

Bring all terms to one side:

\[
5p^3-20p=0.
\]

Factor:

\[
5p(p^2-4)=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
p=0,\quad p=2,\quad p=-2.
\]

If \(p=0\), then \(P=(0,0)\), the vertex case. If \(p=2\), then:

\[
P=(5(2)^2,10(2))=(20,20).
\]

If \(p=-2\), then:

\[
P=(20,-20).
\]

The point \(P\) lies in the first quadrant, so:

\[
\boxed{p=2}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{P=(20,20).}
\]

### Equation of the actual normal

Substitute \(p=2\):

\[
2x+y=10(2)+5(2)^3.
\]

Compute:

\[
10(2)=20,\qquad (2)^3=8,\qquad 5(2)^3=40.
\]

So:

\[
2x+y=20+40=60.
\]

Thus:

\[
y=60-2x.
\]

### Area of the shaded region

The shaded region is bounded by:

- the curve \(C:y^2=20x\);
- the normal line \(2x+y=60\);
- the \(x\)-axis.

The upper branch of the parabola is:

\[
y=\sqrt{20x}.
\]

The curve part runs from \(x=0\) to \(x=20\), because \(P=(20,20)\).

So the area under the curve from \(x=0\) to \(x=20\) is:

\[
A_1=\int_0^{20}\sqrt{20x}\,dx.
\]

Rewrite:

\[
\sqrt{20x}=\sqrt{20}\sqrt{x}.
\]

So:

\[
A_1=\sqrt{20}\int_0^{20}x^{1/2}\,dx.
\]

Integrate:

\[
\int x^{1/2}\,dx=\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}=\frac23x^{3/2}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
A_1=\sqrt{20}\left[\frac23x^{3/2}\right]_0^{20}.
\]

Substitute bounds:

\[
A_1=\sqrt{20}\left(\frac23(20)^{3/2}-\frac23(0)^{3/2}\right).
\]

Since \(0^{3/2}=0\):

\[
A_1=\sqrt{20}\cdot\frac23(20)^{3/2}.
\]

Now:

\[
20^{3/2}=20\sqrt{20}.
\]

So:

\[
A_1=\sqrt{20}\cdot\frac23\cdot20\sqrt{20}.
\]

Combine the square roots:

\[
\sqrt{20}\sqrt{20}=20.
\]

Therefore:

\[
A_1=\frac23\cdot20\cdot20=\frac{800}{3}.
\]

The triangular part \(A_2\), from \(x=20\) to \(x=30\), has base \(10\) and height \(20\). Thus:

\[
A_2=\frac12\times10\times20=100.
\]

The total shaded area is:

\[
A=A_1+A_2=\frac{800}{3}+100.
\]

Write \(100=\frac{300}{3}\):

\[
A=\frac{800}{3}+\frac{300}{3}=\frac{1100}{3}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{A=\frac{1100}{3}.}
\]

## 8.25 Problem Solving with a Parabola Normal, Directrix and Triangle Area

Let:

\[
C:\ y^2=4ax,\qquad a>0.
\]

Let:

\[
P(ap^2,2ap)
\]

be a point on \(C\).

The normal at \(P\) is:

\[
\boxed{y+px=ap(p^2+2).}
\]

Let this normal meet the \(x\)-axis at \(A\), the directrix at \(B\), and let \(F\) be the focus of \(C\).

Suppose the \(x\)-coordinate of \(A\) is \(4a\), and the area of triangle \(ABF\) is \(270\sqrt2\). Find \(a\).

### Step 1: Coordinates of \(A\)

At the \(x\)-axis, \(y=0\). Substitute into the normal:

\[
0+px=ap(p^2+2).
\]

Assuming \(p\ne0\), divide by \(p\):

\[
x=a(p^2+2).
\]

Thus:

\[
A=\bigl(a(p^2+2),0\bigr).
\]

The question gives \(x_A=4a\), so:

\[
a(p^2+2)=4a.
\]

Since \(a>0\), divide by \(a\):

\[
p^2+2=4.
\]

Subtract \(2\):

\[
p^2=2.
\]

If \(P\) is on the upper branch, then \(p=\sqrt2\).

### Step 2: Coordinates of \(B\)

The directrix is \(x=-a\). Substitute \(x=-a\) into the normal:

\[
y+p(-a)=ap(p^2+2).
\]

So:

\[
y-ap=ap(p^2+2).
\]

Add \(ap\):

\[
y=ap(p^2+2)+ap=ap(p^2+3).
\]

Thus:

\[
B=\bigl(-a,\ ap(p^2+3)\bigr).
\]

### Step 3: Coordinates of \(F\)

The focus is:

\[
F=(a,0).
\]

### Step 4: Use \(p^2=2\)

Taking \(p=\sqrt2\), the \(y\)-coordinate of \(B\) is:

\[
ap(p^2+3)=a\sqrt2(2+3)=5a\sqrt2.
\]

So:

\[
B=(-a,5a\sqrt2),\qquad A=(4a,0),\qquad F=(a,0).
\]

### Step 5: Area of triangle \(ABF\)

The base \(AF\) lies on the \(x\)-axis. Its length is:

\[
AF=4a-a=3a.
\]

The perpendicular height from \(B\) to the \(x\)-axis is \(5a\sqrt2\).

So:

\[
\text{Area}=\frac12\times 3a\times 5a\sqrt2.
\]

Thus:

\[
\text{Area}=\frac{15}{2}a^2\sqrt2.
\]

Given:

\[
\frac{15}{2}a^2\sqrt2=270\sqrt2.
\]

Divide by \(\sqrt2\):

\[
\frac{15}{2}a^2=270.
\]

Multiply by \(2\):

\[
15a^2=540.
\]

Divide by \(15\):

\[
a^2=36.
\]

Since \(a>0\):

\[
\boxed{a=6.}
\]

The transcript wording appears to say, after obtaining \(a^2=36\), that \(a=36\). Mathematically the correct conclusion is \(a=6\), because \(a>0\). This lesson preserves the route but corrects the final arithmetic conclusion with the error flagged.

## 8.27 Rectangular Hyperbola Tangent, Axes and Area Problem

Let:

\[
H:\ xy=16.
\]

Compare with \(xy=c^2\). So \(c^2=16\), and since \(c>0\), \(c=4\). Keep \(c\) general until the area condition tells us its value.

A general point is:

\[
P\left(ct,\frac ct\right).
\]

The tangent is:

\[
x+t^2y=2ct.
\]

Let this tangent meet the \(x\)-axis at \(A\), and the \(y\)-axis at \(B\).

### Coordinates of \(A\)

On the \(x\)-axis, \(y=0\). Substitute:

\[
x+t^2(0)=2ct.
\]

So:

\[
x=2ct.
\]

Thus:

\[
A=(2ct,0).
\]

Therefore \(OA=2ct\) when \(A\) lies on the positive \(x\)-axis.

### Coordinates of \(B\)

On the \(y\)-axis, \(x=0\). Substitute:

\[
0+t^2y=2ct.
\]

So:

\[
t^2y=2ct.
\]

Divide by \(t^2\), with \(t\ne0\):

\[
y=\frac{2ct}{t^2}=\frac{2c}{t}.
\]

Thus:

\[
B=\left(0,\frac{2c}{t}\right).
\]

Therefore \(OB=\frac{2c}{t}\) when \(B\) lies on the positive \(y\)-axis.

### Use \(OB=2OA\)

\[
\frac{2c}{t}=2(2ct).
\]

So:

\[
\frac{2c}{t}=4ct.
\]

Multiply by \(t\):

\[
2c=4ct^2.
\]

Divide by \(2c\), with \(c>0\):

\[
1=2t^2.
\]

So:

\[
t^2=\frac12.
\]

Thus:

\[
t=\pm\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]

Since the point \(P\) is in the first quadrant, \(t>0\), so:

\[
\boxed{t=\frac1{\sqrt2}.}
\]

### Use the area of the triangle

The region bounded by the axes and the tangent is a right-angled triangle.

Its area is:

\[
\frac12(OA)(OB).
\]

Substitute:

\[
OA=2ct,\qquad OB=\frac{2c}{t}.
\]

Then:

\[
\text{Area}
=
\frac12(2ct)\left(\frac{2c}{t}\right)
=
\frac12\cdot\frac{4c^2t}{t}
=
2c^2.
\]

Given \(\text{Area}=32\), we get:

\[
2c^2=32.
\]

Divide by \(2\):

\[
c^2=16.
\]

Since \(c>0\):

\[
c=4.
\]

### Coordinates of \(P\)

Now \(P\left(ct,\frac ct\right)\), with \(c=4,\ t=\frac1{\sqrt2}\).

So:

\[
x=ct=4\left(\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)=\frac4{\sqrt2}=\frac{4\sqrt2}{2}=2\sqrt2.
\]

Now:

\[
y=\frac ct=\frac4{1/\sqrt2}=4\sqrt2.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{P=(2\sqrt2,4\sqrt2).}
\]

## 8.28 Locus of Midpoints on a Parabola

Let \(O=(0,0)\), and let \(P\) be a point on \(y^2=4ax\). Use:

\[
P=(at^2,2at).
\]

Let \(M\) be the midpoint of \(OP\). Then:

\[
M=
\left(
\frac{0+at^2}{2},
\frac{0+2at}{2}
\right)
=
\left(
\frac12at^2,
at
\right).
\]

Let \(M=(X,Y)\). Then:

\[
X=\frac12at^2,
\]

\[
Y=at.
\]

Eliminate \(t\).

From \(Y=at\):

\[
t=\frac{Y}{a}.
\]

Substitute into \(X=\frac12at^2\):

\[
X=\frac12a\left(\frac{Y}{a}\right)^2.
\]

Square:

\[
\left(\frac{Y}{a}\right)^2=\frac{Y^2}{a^2}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
X=\frac12a\cdot\frac{Y^2}{a^2}.
\]

Cancel one \(a\):

\[
X=\frac{Y^2}{2a}.
\]

Multiply by \(2a\):

\[
2aX=Y^2.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{Y^2=2aX.}
\]

So the locus of the midpoints is another parabola.

**Bridge Note:** This is ordinary midpoint formula plus parametric elimination. The Further Maths upgrade is recognising that the moving midpoint itself traces out a new curve.

## 8.29 Test Your Understanding: Tangent to a Parabola and the Directrix

Let:

\[
C:\ y^2=4ax,\qquad a>0.

Let:

Q(aq^2,2aq).

\boxed{yq=x+aq^2.}

\[
x=aq^2,\qquad y=2aq.
\]

Differentiate:

\[
\frac{dx}{dq}=2aq,\qquad \frac{dy}{dq}=2a.

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{2a}{2aq}
=
\frac1q.
\]

Thus the tangent gradient at \(Q\) is \(\frac1q\).

\[
y-2aq=\frac1q(x-aq^2).
\]

Multiply by \(q\):

\[
q(y-2aq)=x-aq^2.
\]

Expand:

\[
qy-2aq^2=x-aq^2.
\]

Add \(2aq^2\):

\[
qy=x+aq^2.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{yq=x+aq^2.}
\]

### Part (b): The tangent meets the \(y\)-axis at \(R\). Find the line through \(R\) perpendicular to the tangent

On the \(y\)-axis, \(x=0\). The tangent is \(yq=x+aq^2\). Substitute \(x=0\):

\[
yq=aq^2.
\]

Divide by \(q\), assuming \(q\ne0\):

\[
y=aq.
\]

Thus:

\[
R=(0,aq).
\]

\[
y-aq=-q(x-0).
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{y-aq=-qx.}
\]

Equivalently:

\[
\boxed{y=aq-qx.}
\]

The focus of \(y^2=4ax\) is \((a,0)\). Substitute \(x=a\) into \(y=aq-qx\):

\[
y=aq-q(a)=aq-aq=0.
\]

Therefore \((a,0)\) lies on \(l\), so \(l\) passes through the focus.

### Part (d): Find where \(l\) meets the directrix

The directrix is \(x=-a\).

Use \(y=aq-qx\). Substitute \(x=-a\):

\[
y=aq-q(-a)=aq+aq=2aq.
\]

Thus the intersection with the directrix is:

\[
\boxed{(-a,2aq).}

## 8.30 Exam Question: Normal to a Parabola Meets the Curve Again

Let:

\[
C:\ y^2=4ax.
\]

Let:

\[
P(ap^2,2ap)
\]

and

\[
Q(aq^2,2aq)
\]

be points on \(C\), where the normal at \(P\) passes through \(Q\).

Let \(O=(0,0)\), and suppose:

\[
OP\perp OQ.
\]

Show that:

\[
p^2=2.
\]

### Step 1: Use \(OP\perp OQ\)

The gradient of \(OP\) is:

\[
m_{OP}=\frac{2ap}{ap^2}.
\]

Cancel \(a\):

\[
m_{OP}=\frac{2p}{p^2}.
\]

Cancel one \(p\), assuming \(p\ne0\):

\[
m_{OP}=\frac2p.
\]

The gradient of \(OQ\) is:

\[
m_{OQ}=\frac{2aq}{aq^2}=\frac2q.
\]

Since \(OP\perp OQ\):

\[
m_{OP}m_{OQ}=-1.
\]

So:

\[
\frac2p\cdot\frac2q=-1.
\]

Multiply:

\[
\frac4{pq}=-1.
\]

Multiply by \(pq\):

\[
4=-pq.
\]

Therefore:

\[
pq=-4,
\]

so:

\[
q=-\frac4p.
\]

### Step 2: Equation of the normal at \(P\)

From Section 8.19, the normal at \(P(ap^2,2ap)\) is:

\[
y+px=ap(p^2+2).
\]

Since the normal passes through \(Q(aq^2,2aq)\), substitute:

\[
x=aq^2,\qquad y=2aq.
\]

Then:

\[
2aq+p(aq^2)=ap(p^2+2).
\]

Expand:

\[
2aq+apq^2=ap^3+2ap.
\]

Divide by \(a\), since \(a>0\):

\[
2q+pq^2=p^3+2p.
\]

### Step 3: Substitute \(q=-\dfrac4p\)

\[
2q=2\left(-\frac4p\right)=-\frac8p.
\]

Also:

\[
q^2=\left(-\frac4p\right)^2=\frac{16}{p^2}.
\]

So:

\[
pq^2=p\cdot\frac{16}{p^2}=\frac{16}{p}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
2q+pq^2=-\frac8p+\frac{16}{p}=\frac8p.
\]

The equation becomes:

\[
\frac8p=p^3+2p.
\]

Multiply by \(p\):

\[
8=p^4+2p^2.
\]

Bring all terms to one side:

\[
p^4+2p^2-8=0.
\]

Factorise:

\[
p^4+2p^2-8=(p^2-2)(p^2+4).
\]

So:

\[
(p^2-2)(p^2+4)=0.
\]

The second equation gives \(p^2=-4\), which has no real solution. Thus:

\[
\boxed{p^2=2.}

Factorise:

\[
p^2-3p-28=(p-7)(p+4).
\]

So:

\[
p=7
\]

or:

\[
p=-4.
\]

These correspond to the two tangent points.

For \(p=7\):

\[
x=p^2=49,\qquad y=4p=28.
\]

For \(p=-4\):

\[
x=p^2=16,\qquad y=4p=-16.
\]

Thus:

\[
P=(49,28),\qquad Q=(16,-16),\qquad R=(-28,6).
\]

### Area of \(\triangle PQR\)

Use the coordinate-area determinant formula:

\[
\text{Area}
=
\frac12
\left|
x_1(y_2-y_3)+x_2(y_3-y_1)+x_3(y_1-y_2)
\right|.
\]

Let:

\[
(x_1,y_1)=(49,28),
\]

\[
(x_2,y_2)=(16,-16),
\]

\[
(x_3,y_3)=(-28,6).
\]

Substitute:

\[
\text{Area}
=
\frac12
\left|
49((-16)-6)+16(6-28)+(-28)(28-(-16))
\right|.
\]

Simplify each bracket:

\[
(-16)-6=-22,
\]

\[
6-28=-22,
\]

\[
28-(-16)=44.
\]

So:

\[
\text{Area}
=
\frac12
\left|
49(-22)+16(-22)+(-28)(44)
\right|.
\]

Compute:

\[
49(-22)=-1078,
\]

\[
16(-22)=-352,
\]

\[
(-28)(44)=-1232.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\text{Area}
=
\frac12
\left|
-1078-352-1232
\right|.
\]

Add:

\[
-1078-352=-1430,
\]

\[
-1430-1232=-2662.
\]

Thus:

\[
\text{Area}
=
\frac12| -2662 |
=
\frac12(2662)
=
1331.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Area of }\triangle PQR=1331.}
\]

## 8.32 Locus from a Chord Through a Fixed Point

Let:

\[
C:\ y^2=16x.
\]

Use:

\[
P=(4p^2,8p),\qquad Q=(4q^2,8q).
\]

Let \(A\) be the intersection point of the tangents at \(P\) and \(Q\). The tangent to \(y^2=16x\) at parameter \(p\), using \(a=4\), is:

\[
py=x+4p^2.
\]

The tangent at \(Q\) is:

\[
qy=x+4q^2.
\]

At their intersection \(A=(X,Y)\), both are true:

\[
pY=X+4p^2,
\]

\[
qY=X+4q^2.
\]

Subtract the second equation from the first:

\[
pY-qY=(X+4p^2)-(X+4q^2).
\]

The \(X\)-terms cancel:

\[
(p-q)Y=4p^2-4q^2.
\]

Factor:

\[
(p-q)Y=4(p^2-q^2).
\]

Use:

\[
p^2-q^2=(p-q)(p+q).
\]

So:

\[
(p-q)Y=4(p-q)(p+q).
\]

Since \(p\ne q\), divide by \(p-q\):

\[
Y=4(p+q).
\]

Substitute into:

\[
pY=X+4p^2.
\]

Then:

\[
p\cdot4(p+q)=X+4p^2.
\]

So:

\[
4p(p+q)=X+4p^2.
\]

Expand:

\[
4p^2+4pq=X+4p^2.
\]

Subtract \(4p^2\):

\[
4pq=X.
\]

Therefore:

\[
A=(4pq,4(p+q)).
\]

Now use the chord condition. The chord \(PQ\) passes through \((8,0)\).

The gradient of \(PQ\) is:

\[
m_{PQ}
=
\frac{8q-8p}{4q^2-4p^2}.
\]

Factor:

\[
m_{PQ}
=
\frac{8(q-p)}{4(q^2-p^2)}.
\]

Use:

\[
q^2-p^2=(q-p)(q+p).
\]

So:

\[
m_{PQ}
=
\frac{8(q-p)}{4(q-p)(q+p)}.
\]

Cancel \(q-p\):

\[
m_{PQ}=\frac{8}{4(q+p)}=\frac{2}{p+q}.
\]

The line through \(P(4p^2,8p)\) has equation:

\[
y-8p=\frac{2}{p+q}(x-4p^2).
\]

Since it passes through \((8,0)\), substitute \(x=8,\ y=0\):

\[
0-8p=\frac{2}{p+q}(8-4p^2).
\]

So:

\[
-8p=\frac{2(8-4p^2)}{p+q}.
\]

Multiply by \(p+q\):

\[
-8p(p+q)=2(8-4p^2).
\]

Expand:

\[
-8p^2-8pq=16-8p^2.
\]

Add \(8p^2\):

\[
-8pq=16.
\]

Divide by \(-8\):

\[
pq=-2.
\]

Now \(A=(4pq,4(p+q))\). Therefore:

\[
X=4pq=4(-2)=-8.
\]

So every such point \(A\) has:

\[
\boxed{X=-8.}
\]

Thus the locus is the vertical line:

\[
\boxed{x=-8.}
\]

# 12. Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

## 12.1 Symbol Role Confusion

| Symbol | Role | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| \(x,y\) | Variables or coordinates | Treating \(x,y\) as fixed when deriving a whole curve. |
| \(t,p,q\) | Parameters | Treating a parameter as a constant that fixes the curve. |
| \(a,c\) | Constants | Treating \(a\) or \(c\) as the parameter that chooses a point. |
| \(P,Q,R,A,B,F,S\) | Points | Forgetting to define each point before using it. |

## 12.2 Horizontal Parabola Trap

Ordinary Maths trains you to think \(y=ax^2+bx+c\). Here the core parabola is \(y^2=4ax\). This is not generally a function \(y=f(x)\). For \(x>0\):

\[
y=\pm2\sqrt{ax}.
\]

Trap:

\[
y=\sqrt{4ax}
\]

only gives the upper branch.

## 12.3 Directrix Sign Trap

For \(y^2=4ax\), the focus is \((a,0)\), but the directrix is \(x=-a\). A common mistake is to write \(x=a\).

## 12.4 Distance to the Directrix Trap

The distance from \(P(x,y)\) to \(x=-a\) is:

\[
x-(-a)=x+a,
\]

not \(x-a\).

## 12.5 Squaring Trap

When converting \(y=2at\) to \(t=\frac{y}{2a}\), remember:

\[
\left(\frac{y}{2a}\right)^2=\frac{y^2}{4a^2}.
\]

## 12.6 Hyperbola Name Trap

What you previously called a reciprocal graph is formally a rectangular hyperbola in this topic.

## 12.7 \(t=0\) Trap for Rectangular Hyperbolas

For \(x=ct,\ y=\frac ct\), we must have \(t\ne0\). If \(t=0\), then \(y=\frac c0\) is undefined.

## 12.8 Tangent vs Normal Trap

For the parabola \(x=at^2,\ y=2at\):

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1t.
\]

That is the tangent gradient. The normal gradient is:

\[
-t.
\]

## 12.9 Parametric Differentiation Trap

Use:

\[
\frac{dy}{dx}
=
\frac{\frac{dy}{dt}}{\frac{dx}{dt}},
\]

not the reciprocal.

## 12.10 Known Root or Known Factor Trap

If a line meets a conic at a known point and one other point, the known point gives a known root. Example: \(P(8,-8)\) on \(y^2=8x\) gave one root \(y=-8\), so the quadratic had factor \(y+8\).

## 12.11 Area Trap

When a triangle is not sitting neatly with a horizontal base, you may still use:

\[
\frac12\times\text{base}\times\text{perpendicular height}.
\]

The height is the perpendicular distance from the vertex to the extended base, not necessarily a side drawn in the original diagram.

## 12.12 Evidence Typo and Transcript Slip Trap

Two evidence issues are flagged:

1. The rectangular hyperbola tangent derivation requires \(t^2y\), not \(y^2\).
2. One transcript route appears to conclude \(a=36\) after deriving \(a^2=36\). The correct conclusion, since \(a>0\), is \(a=6\).

# 15. Exam Technique Notes

## 15.1 Start with the Standard Form

For a parabola, compare with:

\[
y^2=4ax.
\]

For a rectangular hyperbola, compare with:

\[
xy=c^2.
\]

Do this before sketching, differentiating or solving simultaneous equations.

## 15.2 Write the General Point Early

For a parabola:

\[
P(at^2,2at).
\]

For a rectangular hyperbola:

\[
P\left(ct,\frac ct\right).
\]

When a question uses two points, write:

\[
P(ap^2,2ap),\qquad Q(aq^2,2aq)
\]

or:

\[
P\left(cp,\frac cp\right),\qquad Q\left(cq,\frac cq\right).
\]

## 15.3 Use the Given Geometry Immediately

If a normal meets the \(x\)-axis, set \(y=0\).

If it meets the directrix of \(y^2=4ax\), set:

\[
x=-a.
\]

If the focus is mentioned, write:

\[
F=(a,0).
\]

If a point lies on \(xy=c^2\), use \(xy=c^2\) or \(x=ct,\ y=\frac ct\).

## 15.4 Choose Cartesian or Parametric Method Wisely

Use Cartesian form when:

- a specific \(x\)- or \(y\)-coordinate is given;
- the curve is simple to rearrange;
- a line equation is already in \(x,y\);
- you only need one or two intersection points.

Use parametric form when:

- the point is given as \((at^2,2at)\) or \((ct,c/t)\);
- the question asks for a general tangent or normal;
- the algebra is cleaner in \(t,p,q\);
- two points on the same conic are involved.

## 15.5 Do Not Memorise Formulae Without Derivation

You may use these formulae:

\[
\text{Tangent to }y^2=4ax:\quad ty=x+at^2,
\]

\[
\text{Normal to }y^2=4ax:\quad y+tx=2at+at^3,
\]

\[
\text{Tangent to }xy=c^2:\quad x+t^2y=2ct.
\]

But many questions award marks for deriving them. The safest exam habit is:

1. write the parametric equations;
2. differentiate;
3. use point-gradient form;
4. simplify carefully.

## 15.6 Keep Exact Values

Do not turn exact answers such as:

\[
\sqrt2,\quad 2\sqrt2,\quad \frac{17}{4},\quad \frac{1100}{3}
\]

into decimals unless the question explicitly asks for a decimal approximation.

## 15.7 Sketch Before Heavy Algebra

A quick sketch helps decide:

- which branch a point is on;
- whether \(p>0\) or \(p<0\);
- whether a second intersection is on the upper or lower branch;
- whether a normal/tangent should have positive or negative gradient;
- whether an answer such as \(Q=(1/2,2)\) is plausible.

## 15.8 Watch for Preview Material

Ellipse, full hyperbola theory, eccentricity beyond basic interpretation, and hyperbolic functions are preview material in this evidence. This first conics chapter focuses on the parabola and rectangular hyperbola, with ellipse and general hyperbola completed later.

# 19. Final Student Checklist

## 19.1 Prerequisite Confidence Checklist

Before moving on, check that you can:

- [ ] solve simultaneous equations involving a line and a quadratic;
- [ ] calculate a midpoint;
- [ ] calculate a gradient;
- [ ] find the equation of a line through two points;
- [ ] find a tangent using differentiation;
- [ ] find a normal using the negative reciprocal gradient;
- [ ] use exact fractions and surds confidently;
- [ ] use Pythagoras’ theorem and the distance formula;
- [ ] recognise \(y=k/x\) as a reciprocal-style graph;
- [ ] understand a parameter as a variable that generates points.

## 19.2 Further Maths Method Checklist

You should now be able to:

- [ ] explain what a conic section is;
- [ ] name circle, ellipse, parabola and hyperbola as conic families;
- [ ] explain why this lesson focuses on parabolas and rectangular hyperbolas;
- [ ] convert \(x=at^2,\ y=2at\) into \(y^2=4ax\);
- [ ] convert \(x=ct,\ y=c/t\) into \(xy=c^2\);
- [ ] state the focus of \(y^2=4ax\) as \((a,0)\);
- [ ] state the directrix of \(y^2=4ax\) as \(x=-a\);
- [ ] state the vertex of \(y^2=4ax\) as \((0,0)\);
- [ ] state the axis of symmetry of \(y^2=4ax\) as \(y=0\);
- [ ] use \(P(at^2,2at)\) as a general point on a parabola;
- [ ] use \(P(ct,c/t)\) as a general point on a rectangular hyperbola;
- [ ] derive the tangent to \(y^2=4ax\): \(ty=x+at^2\);
- [ ] derive the normal to \(y^2=4ax\): \(y+tx=2at+at^3\);
- [ ] derive the tangent to \(xy=c^2\): \(x+t^2y=2ct\);
- [ ] use the normal to \(xy=c^2\): \(p^3x-py+c(1-p^4)=0\);
- [ ] solve conic intersection questions using Cartesian or parametric methods;
- [ ] solve locus questions by naming the moving point \((X,Y)\) and eliminating the parameter.

## 19.3 Exam Technique Checklist

In an exam-style conics question, remember to:

- [ ] compare with \(y^2=4ax\) or \(xy=c^2\) immediately;
- [ ] write the general point early;
- [ ] define every parameter before using it;
- [ ] sketch the conic before heavy algebra;
- [ ] decide whether Cartesian or parametric form is cleaner;
- [ ] use known points as known roots;
- [ ] use \(y=0\) for the \(x\)-axis;
- [ ] use \(x=0\) for the \(y\)-axis;
- [ ] use \(x=-a\) for the directrix of \(y^2=4ax\);
- [ ] keep exact values unless decimals are requested;
- [ ] check the branch or quadrant before choosing a sign;
- [ ] show enough working when deriving tangent and normal formulae.

## 19.4 Bridge Checklist

You should be able to explain:

- [ ] how \(y=ax^2\) relates to \(y^2=4ax\);
- [ ] why \(y^2=4ax\) is a horizontal parabola;
- [ ] why \(y^2=4ax\) is not generally \(y=f(x)\);
- [ ] how \(y=k/x\) relates to \(xy=c^2\);
- [ ] why a rectangular hyperbola has asymptotes \(x=0\) and \(y=0\);
- [ ] how ordinary differentiation supports tangent and normal work;
- [ ] how ordinary coordinate geometry still powers conic problems.

## 19.5 Diagram and Visual Understanding Checklist

You should be able to identify on a diagram:

- [ ] a right-opening parabola;
- [ ] the vertex \((0,0)\);
- [ ] the focus \((a,0)\);
- [ ] the directrix \(x=-a\);
- [ ] the axis of symmetry \(y=0\);
- [ ] a general point \(P(at^2,2at)\);
- [ ] equal distances from \(P\) to the focus and directrix;
- [ ] a rectangular hyperbola \(xy=c^2\);
- [ ] asymptotes \(x=0\) and \(y=0\);
- [ ] tangent and normal lines at a point;
- [ ] a chord and its midpoint;
- [ ] a locus traced by a moving point.
