# 4. Learning Objectives

## 4.1 Core enrichment objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. recognise when a rational inequality contains a denominator whose sign is not known;
2. avoid unsafe multiplication by expressions involving \(x\);
3. multiply by squared denominator factors such as \((x-2)^2\), \((x+1)^2(x+3)^2\), or \(x^2\), because these are non-negative and, away from excluded values, positive;
4. rearrange a rational inequality to one side;
5. factorise rather than expand where common factors are visible;
6. find critical values by solving the related equality;
7. use a simple sketch or sign structure to identify where the expression is \(<0\), \(\le 0\), \(>0\), or \(\ge 0\);
8. exclude values that make the original expression undefined;
9. express solution sets accurately using inequalities or set notation;
10. solve graphical inequalities by sketching both curves, finding intersections, reading vertical order and accounting for asymptotes;
11. solve modulus inequalities by sketching \(|f(x)|\), considering positive and negative branches, and rejecting phantom critical values.

## 4.2 Bridge objectives

You should connect this lesson to ordinary A-Level Mathematics by being able to:

1. recall how ordinary quadratic inequalities are solved by rearranging, factorising and sketching;
2. explain how the same idea extends to cubic, quartic and rational inequalities;
3. recall that multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number reverses the inequality sign;
4. explain why multiplying by a variable expression is dangerous unless its sign is known;
5. recall the graph of \(y=\frac{1}{x}\), straight lines, quadratics and simple modulus graphs;
6. recognise that \(|f(x)|\) reflects the negative part of \(y=f(x)\) above the \(x\)-axis.

## 4.3 Exam technique objectives

You should be able to:

1. recognise the command “use algebra” and show algebraic solving, rather than relying only on calculator output;
2. use a graphics calculator or graphing tool as a check, not as a replacement for required algebra;
3. write exact critical values such as \(2\pm\sqrt7\), not decimal approximations, unless decimals are requested;
4. use decimal approximations only as a private checking tool to locate roots on a sketch;
5. include or exclude endpoints according to \(<,\le,>,\ge\) and according to whether the original expression is defined.

# 5. Explicit Prerequisite Recap

## 5.1 GCSE foundations

You should already be confident with solving linear equations, expanding and factorising brackets, factorising quadratics, solving quadratic equations, understanding inequalities, interpreting number-line intervals, plotting simple graphs and recognising that division by zero is not defined.

## 5.2 Ordinary AS/A2 Mathematics foundations

You should already have seen linear and quadratic inequalities, graph sketches of quadratics, cubics and reciprocal graphs, solving equations by finding points of intersection, modulus notation \(|x|\) and graphs involving \(|f(x)|\), transformations and reflections of graphs, and set notation in simple contexts.

The lesson PDF begins with an ordinary “Pure Year 1” recap of solving a quadratic inequality such as

\[
2x^2<x+3.
\]

The steps are:

\[
2x^2-x-3<0,
\]

\[
(2x-3)(x+1)<0,
\]

so the critical values are

\[
x=-1,\qquad x=\frac32.
\]

A sketch of the upward-opening quadratic then gives

\[
-1<x<\frac32.
\]

This ordinary method is the seed. The Further Pure enrichment version grows a few extra teeth: rational expressions, excluded values, asymptotes, modulus branches and higher-degree sign sketches.

## 5.3 A-Level Maths Bridge: What You Already Know and What Further Maths Changes

In ordinary A-Level Maths, this idea appeared as solving quadratic inequalities by finding where a graph is above or below the \(x\)-axis.

In Further Maths enrichment, the same idea becomes a wider toolkit for rational, graphical and modulus inequalities.

The key upgrade is that you must protect the inequality sign from unknown signs by multiplying by squares of denominators and then using sketches to interpret the resulting expression.

The danger is that an algebraic move that feels harmless, such as multiplying by \(x\), can quietly turn the whole answer inside out.

# 6. Big Picture Explanation

An equation asks:

\[
\text{Where are two expressions equal?}
\]

An inequality asks:

\[
\text{Where is one expression bigger, smaller, above, below, positive or negative?}
\]

That one change makes the problem much richer. An equation often gives points. An inequality gives regions.

For example,

\[
\frac1x=x
\]

asks where the reciprocal curve and the line meet. But

\[
\frac1x>x
\]

asks where the reciprocal curve is above the line. The answer is not just a point. It is a pair of intervals.

The main storyline of this lesson is:

\[
\text{unsafe inequality}\to\text{multiply by a positive square}\to\text{polynomial inequality}\to\text{critical values}\to\text{sketch}\to\text{solution intervals}.
\]

For graphical inequalities, the storyline becomes:

\[
\text{sketch both graphs}\to\text{find intersections}\to\text{include asymptotes and domain restrictions}\to\text{read where one graph is above or below the other}.
\]

For modulus inequalities, the storyline becomes:

\[
|f(x)|\to\text{reflect negative parts upward}\to\text{solve positive and negative branch equations}\to\text{use the graph to reject phantom roots}\to\text{write intervals}.
\]

# 7. Key Definitions and Notation

## 7.1 Inequality symbols

For real numbers or real-valued expressions:

\[
a<b,\qquad a\le b,\qquad a>b,\qquad a\ge b.
\]

Strict inequalities \(<\) and \(>\) exclude endpoints. Weak inequalities \(\le\) and \(\ge\) include endpoints unless the original expression is undefined there.

## 7.2 Solution set

A solution set is the set of all values of the variable that satisfy the inequality.

For example,

\[
x<-1\quad\text{or}\quad 0<x<1
\]

can be written in set notation as

\[
\{x:x<-1\}\cup\{x:0<x<1\}.
\]

## 7.3 Undefined values

A value is undefined if it causes division by zero.

For example,

\[
\frac{x^2}{x-2}
\]

is undefined when \(x=2\). Therefore any solution set for an inequality involving \(\frac{x^2}{x-2}\) must exclude \(x=2\), even if a later sketch seems to include it.

## 7.4 Critical values

Critical values are the values of \(x\) where the sign or comparison can change. They usually come from solving the equality formed from the inequality, finding where a transformed expression equals zero, finding graph intersections, or noting values where the original expression is undefined.

## 7.5 Safe multiplication by squared denominators

If \(d(x)\ne0\), then

\[
(d(x))^2>0.
\]

This means multiplying both sides of an inequality by \((d(x))^2\) does **not** reverse the inequality sign.

## 7.6 Asymptote

An asymptote is a line that a graph approaches but does not meet in the relevant direction. For

\[
y=\frac{7x}{3x+1},
\]

the vertical asymptote is \(x=-\frac13\), and the horizontal asymptote is \(y=\frac73\).

## 7.7 Modulus

\[
|a|=\begin{cases}a,&a\ge0,\\-a,&a<0.\end{cases}
\]

For a function \(f(x)\), \(|f(x)|\) keeps the parts of \(y=f(x)\) that are already above or on the \(x\)-axis and reflects the parts below the \(x\)-axis above the \(x\)-axis.

# 8. Core Theory

## 8.1 Why ordinary inequality operations are not enough

In ordinary algebra, if you see

\[
\frac1x>x,
\]

your equation-solving instinct might whisper: “Multiply by \(x\).” That instinct is dangerous here.

If we multiplied by \(x\), we might write

\[
1>x^2,
\]

then \(-1<x<1\). But this is wrong for the original inequality \(\frac1x>x\), \(x\ne0\), because \(x\) might be positive or negative.

**Bridge Note:** In ordinary A-Level Maths, you learned that multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses an inequality. Here, Further Maths-style enrichment extends this by forcing you to ask whether a whole expression, such as \(x\), \(x-2\), or \(3x+1\), is positive or negative.

## 8.2 The denominator-squared method

For an inequality involving rational expressions:

1. Identify values that make denominators zero.
2. State or remember that those values are excluded.
3. Multiply by the square of every denominator factor needed.
4. Cancel carefully.
5. Rearrange to one side.
6. Factorise where possible.
7. Find critical values by setting the expression equal to zero.
8. Sketch the resulting polynomial or use a sign chart.
9. Read off the required intervals.
10. Remove any excluded values from the original expression.
11. Write the answer clearly, preferably in set notation if requested.

## 8.3 Core algebraic example: \(\frac1x>x\), \(x\ne0\)

\[
\frac1x>x.
\]

Since \(x\ne0\), \(x^2>0\). Multiply both sides by \(x^2\):

\[
\frac1x\cdot x^2>x\cdot x^2.
\]

Cancel one factor of \(x\) on the left:

\[
x>x^3.
\]

Bring everything to one side:

\[
x^3-x<0.
\]

Find critical values:

\[
x^3-x=0,
\]

\[
x(x^2-1)=0,
\]

\[
x(x+1)(x-1)=0.
\]

So

\[
x=-1,\quad x=0,\quad x=1.
\]

A positive cubic has sign pattern \(-,+,-,+\). We need \(x^3-x<0\), so

\[
x<-1\quad\text{or}\quad 0<x<1.
\]

## 8.4 Factorising before expanding

For

\[
\frac{x^2}{x-2}<x+1,
\]

multiply by \((x-2)^2\):

\[
x^2(x-2)<(x+1)(x-2)^2.
\]

Bring to one side:

\[
x^2(x-2)-(x+1)(x-2)^2<0.
\]

Factorise before expanding:

\[
(x-2)\left[x^2-(x+1)(x-2)\right]<0.
\]

Since

\[
(x+1)(x-2)=x^2-x-2,
\]

we get

\[
x^2-(x^2-x-2)=x+2.
\]

Thus

\[
(x-2)(x+2)<0,
\]

and

\[
-2<x<2.
\]

## 8.5 Multiple denominators

For

\[
\frac{x}{x+1}\le \frac{2}{x+3},\qquad x\ne-1,\quad x\ne-3,
\]

multiply by \((x+1)^2(x+3)^2\):

\[
x(x+1)(x+3)^2\le2(x+1)^2(x+3).
\]

Bring to one side and factorise:

\[
(x+1)(x+3)[x(x+3)-2(x+1)]\le0.
\]

Inside the bracket:

\[
x(x+3)-2(x+1)=x^2+3x-2x-2=x^2+x-2=(x+2)(x-1).
\]

Therefore

\[
(x+1)(x+3)(x+2)(x-1)\le0.
\]

The raw intervals are \(-3\le x\le-2\) or \(-1\le x\le1\). Remove excluded values \(-3\) and \(-1\):

\[
-3<x\le-2\quad\text{or}\quad -1<x\le1.
\]

## 8.6 Positive and negative quartics

A quartic with four simple roots and positive leading coefficient has sign pattern:

\[
+,-,+,-,+.
\]

A quartic with four simple roots and negative leading coefficient has sign pattern:

\[
-,+,-,+,-.
\]

## 8.7 Graphical inequalities

A graphical inequality such as

\[
\frac{7x}{3x+1}<4-x
\]

asks when \(y=\frac{7x}{3x+1}\) is below \(y=4-x\).

For \(y=\frac{7x}{3x+1}\), the vertical asymptote is found by setting \(3x+1=0\), so \(x=-\frac13\). The horizontal asymptote comes from

\[
\frac{7x}{3x+1}=\frac{7}{3+\frac1x}\to\frac73.
\]

Find intersections:

\[
\frac{7x}{3x+1}=4-x,
\]

\[
7x=(4-x)(3x+1),
\]

\[
7x=-3x^2+11x+4,
\]

\[
3x^2-4x-4=0,
\]

\[
(3x+2)(x-2)=0.
\]

Thus

\[
x=-\frac23\quad\text{or}\quad x=2.
\]

The asymptote \(x=-\frac13\) splits the graph. The solution is

\[
x<-\frac23\quad\text{or}\quad -\frac13<x<2.
\]

## 8.8 Modulus inequalities

For

\[
|x^2-4x|<3,
\]

sketch \(y=|x^2-4x|\) and compare it with \(y=3\).

Positive branch:

\[
x^2-4x=3\implies x^2-4x-3=0\implies x=2\pm\sqrt7.
\]

Negative branch:

\[
-(x^2-4x)=3\implies x^2-4x+3=0\implies x=1,3.
\]

The graph gives

\[
2-\sqrt7<x<1\quad\text{or}\quad 3<x<2+\sqrt7.
\]

## 8.9 Modulus with rearrangement first

For

\[
|3x|+x\le2,
\]

isolate the modulus:

\[
|3x|\le2-x.
\]

Positive branch:

\[
3x=2-x\implies x=\frac12.
\]

Negative branch:

\[
-3x=2-x\implies x=-1.
\]

The solution is

\[
-1\le x\le\frac12.
\]

## 8.10 Phantom critical values in modulus problems

For

\[
|x^2-19|\le5(x-1),
\]

positive branch:

\[
x^2-19=5(x-1)\implies x^2-5x-14=0\implies x=-2,7.
\]

negative branch:

\[
-(x^2-19)=5(x-1)\implies x^2+5x-24=0\implies x=-8,3.
\]

The sketch shows that the valid boundaries are \(x=3\) and \(x=7\), so

\[
3\le x\le7.
\]

# 11. Worked Examples

## Worked Example 1: Why \(\frac1x>x\) cannot be solved by multiplying by \(x\)

Solve

\[
\frac1x>x,\qquad x\ne0.
\]

Unsafe approach:

\[
\frac1x>x\Rightarrow 1>x^2\Rightarrow -1<x<1.
\]

This is wrong because the sign of \(x\) is unknown.

Correct method: since \(x\ne0\), \(x^2>0\). Multiply by \(x^2\):

\[
\frac1x\cdot x^2>x\cdot x^2,
\]

\[
x>x^3,
\]

\[
x^3-x<0,
\]

\[
x(x+1)(x-1)<0.
\]

Critical values:

\[
x=-1,0,1.
\]

A positive cubic has sign pattern \(-,+,-,+\), so

\[
\boxed{x<-1\quad\text{or}\quad 0<x<1.}
\]

## Worked Example 2: Solving \(\frac{x^2}{x-2}<x+1\)

\[
\frac{x^2}{x-2}<x+1,\qquad x\ne2.
\]

Since \((x-2)^2>0\), multiply by \((x-2)^2\):

\[
x^2(x-2)<(x+1)(x-2)^2.
\]

Bring to one side:

\[
x^2(x-2)-(x+1)(x-2)^2<0.
\]

Factorise:

\[
(x-2)[x^2-(x+1)(x-2)]<0.
\]

Since

\[
(x+1)(x-2)=x^2-x-2,
\]

\[
x^2-(x^2-x-2)=x+2.
\]

So

\[
(x-2)(x+2)<0.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{-2<x<2.}
\]

## Worked Example 3: Two denominators and set notation

Solve

\[
\frac{x}{x+1}\le\frac2{x+3},\qquad x\ne-1,\quad x\ne-3.
\]

Multiply by \((x+1)^2(x+3)^2\):

\[
x(x+1)(x+3)^2\le2(x+1)^2(x+3).
\]

Bring to one side and factorise:

\[
(x+1)(x+3)[x(x+3)-2(x+1)]\le0.
\]

\[
x(x+3)-2(x+1)=x^2+3x-2x-2=x^2+x-2=(x+2)(x-1).
\]

Thus

\[
(x+1)(x+3)(x+2)(x-1)\le0.
\]

Critical values:

\[
-3,-2,-1,1.
\]

A positive quartic gives intervals \(-3\le x\le-2\) and \(-1\le x\le1\), but \(-3\) and \(-1\) are excluded.

\[
\boxed{\{x:-3<x\le-2\}\cup\{x:-1<x\le1\}.}
\]

## Worked Example 4: “Your Turn” algebraic inequality

Solve

\[
\frac{x+1}{2x-3}<\frac1{x-3}.
\]

The excluded values are \(x=\frac32\) and \(x=3\). Multiply by \((2x-3)^2(x-3)^2\):

\[
(x+1)(2x-3)(x-3)^2<(2x-3)^2(x-3).
\]

Bring to one side and factorise:

\[
(2x-3)(x-3)[(x+1)(x-3)-(2x-3)]<0.
\]

Inside the bracket:

\[
(x+1)(x-3)-(2x-3)=x^2-2x-3-2x+3=x^2-4x=x(x-4).
\]

So

\[
x(2x-3)(x-3)(x-4)<0.
\]

Critical values:

\[
0,\frac32,3,4.
\]

A positive quartic is negative on the second and fourth intervals:

\[
\boxed{0<x<\frac32\quad\text{or}\quad 3<x<4.}
\]

The reciprocal graph is above \(y=1\) on the positive branch between the asymptote \(x=0\) and the intersection \(x=1\):

\boxed{0<x<1.}

## Worked Example 6: Graphical inequality \(\frac{7x}{3x+1}<4-x\)

Vertical asymptote:

\[
3x+1=0\Rightarrow x=-\frac13.
\]

Horizontal asymptote:

\[
\frac{7x}{3x+1}=\frac7{3+\frac1x}\to\frac73.
\]

Intersections:

\[
\frac{7x}{3x+1}=4-x,
\]

\[
7x=(4-x)(3x+1),
\]

\[
3x^2-4x-4=0,
\]

\[
(3x+2)(x-2)=0.
\]

So

\[
x=-\frac23\quad\text{or}\quad x=2.
\]

From the sketch, the rational graph is below the line when

\[
\boxed{x<-\frac23\quad\text{or}\quad -\frac13<x<2.}
\]

## Worked Example 7: Old FP1-style rational inequality

Solve

\[
\frac{x}{x^2-2x-3}\le\frac1{x+3}.
\]

Factorise:

\[
x^2-2x-3=(x-3)(x+1).
\]

Excluded values: \(x=3,-1,-3\). Multiply by squared denominators and factorise:

\[
(x-3)(x+1)(x+3)[x(x+3)-(x-3)(x+1)]\le0.
\]

Inside the bracket:

\[
x(x+3)-(x-3)(x+1)=x^2+3x-(x^2-2x-3)=5x+3.
\]

So

\[
(x-3)(x+1)(x+3)(5x+3)\le0.
\]

Critical values: \(-3,-1,-\frac35,3\). Excluding denominator values gives

\[
\boxed{-3<x<-1\quad\text{or}\quad -\frac35\le x<3.}
\]

## Worked Example 8: Old P4-style rational inequality

Solve

\[
\frac{x^2}{x-2}>2x.
\]

Excluded value: \(x=2\). Multiply by \((x-2)^2\):

\[
x^2(x-2)>2x(x-2)^2.
\]

Bring to one side and factorise:

\[
x(x-2)[x-2(x-2)]>0.
\]

\[
x-2(x-2)=4-x.
\]

So

\[
x(x-2)(4-x)>0.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
x(x-2)(x-4)<0.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{x<0\quad\text{or}\quad 2<x<4.}
\]

## Worked Example 9: Harder AS-style rational inequality

Solve

\[
\frac{x+1}{2x^2+5x-3}>\frac{x}{4x^2-1}.
\]

Factorise denominators:

\[
2x^2+5x-3=(2x-1)(x+3),\qquad 4x^2-1=(2x-1)(2x+1).
\]

Excluded values:

\[
x=\frac12,-3,-\frac12.
\]

After multiplying by squared factors and factorising:

\[
(2x-1)(x+3)(2x+1)[(x+1)(2x+1)-x(x+3)]>0.
\]

Inside the bracket:

\[
(x+1)(2x+1)-x(x+3)=2x^2+3x+1-(x^2+3x)=x^2+1.
\]

So

\[
(2x-1)(x+3)(2x+1)(x^2+1)>0.
\]

Since \(x^2+1>0\), the sign is controlled by the cubic factors. Thus

\[
\boxed{-3<x<-\frac12\quad\text{or}\quad x>\frac12.}
\]

## Worked Example 15: Two modulus distance functions

Particle distances are modelled by

\[
d_A=|5t-31|,
\]

and

\[
d_B=|3t^2-25t+8|,
\]

where \(t\ge0\). We need \(d_A>d_B\):

\[
|5t-31|>|3t^2-25t+8|.
\]

Equality branch 1:

\[
5t-31=3t^2-25t+8\Rightarrow 3t^2-30t+39=0\Rightarrow t=5\pm2\sqrt3.

Equality branch 2:

\[
31-5t=3t^2-25t+8\Rightarrow 3t^2-20t-23=0\Rightarrow t=\frac{23}{3},-1.
\]

Reject \(t=-1\) because \(t\ge0\). From the graph:

\boxed{0\le t<5-2\sqrt3\quad\text{or}\quad \frac{23}{3}<t<5+2\sqrt3.}

## Worked Example 16: Modulus in the denominator

For

\[
f(x)=\frac{x}{|x|-2},
\]

solve

\[
f(x)<2x-5.
\]

Domain restriction:

\[
|x|-2=0\Rightarrow x=\pm2.
\]

For \(x\ge0\), \(|x|=x\), so solve

\[
2x-5=\frac{x}{x-2}.
\]

\[
(2x-5)(x-2)=x\Rightarrow 2x^2-10x+10=0\Rightarrow x=\frac{5\pm\sqrt5}{2}.
\]

For \(x<0\), \(|x|=-x\), so solve

\[
2x-5=\frac{x}{-x-2}.
\]

\[
(2x-5)(-x-2)=x\Rightarrow -2x^2+10=0\Rightarrow x=\pm\sqrt5.
\]

Keep only \(-\sqrt5\) for the \(x<0\) branch. From the sketch:

\[
\boxed{-\sqrt5<x<-2\quad\text{or}\quad \frac{5-\sqrt5}{2}<x<2\quad\text{or}\quad x>\frac{5+\sqrt5}{2}.}
\]

# 12. Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

## 12.1 Multiplying by an expression involving \(x\)

The trap is starting from \(\frac1x>x\), multiplying by \(x\), and writing \(1>x^2\). This is unsafe because \(x\) may be positive or negative. Use \(x^2\), because \(x^2>0\) when \(x\ne0\).

## 12.2 Forgetting excluded denominator values

After transforming a rational inequality, a root may be a denominator zero from the original expression. Such a value is not allowed, even if the transformed inequality has \(\le\) or \(\ge\).

## 12.3 Treating \(\le\) as “include everything”

A value can be included only if it satisfies equality in the transformed expression and is not excluded from the original expression.

## 12.4 Expanding too early

In expressions such as

\[
x^2(x-2)-(x+1)(x-2)^2<0,
\]

factorise the common \((x-2)\) before expanding.

## 12.5 Losing the inequality sign when rearranging

From \(x>x^3\), the correct rearrangement is \(x^3-x<0\), not \(x^3-x>0\).

## 12.6 Writing “and” instead of “or”

Use \(\cup\) or “or” for disjoint intervals:

\[
\{x:x<-1\}\cup\{x:0<x<1\}.
\]

## 12.7 Misreading graphical inequalities

Intersections are not enough. Asymptotes and domain restrictions can split the solution.

## 12.8 Ignoring large-\(x\) and small-\(x\) behaviour

For rational graphs, horizontal asymptotes often come from large positive or negative \(x\) behaviour.

## 12.9 Relying on a graphics calculator when the question says “use algebra”

Use the calculator to check, but show algebraic factorisation or equation solving.

## 12.10 Replacing exact values by decimals

Use decimals only to place roots on sketches. Final answers should keep exact values such as \(2\pm\sqrt7\).

## 12.11 Accepting phantom modulus roots

Modulus branch equations can generate roots that belong to a branch continuation rather than the actual modulus graph. The graph decides.

## 12.12 Forgetting isolated solutions in modulus inequalities

Some modulus inequalities produce an interval plus a single point, such as \(-5\le x\le-3\) or \(x=2\).

## 12.13 Forgetting branch ranges in modulus-denominator problems

Roots from a branch equation must satisfy the branch condition, such as \(x<0\) or \(x\ge0\).

## 12.14 Failing to split around a modulus-denominator asymptote

If \(x=6\) makes a denominator zero, an interval such as \(5<x<\frac{1+\sqrt{241}}2\) must be split into \(5<x<6\) and \(6<x<\frac{1+\sqrt{241}}2\).

## 12.15 Forgetting physical domain restrictions

For modelling questions, \(t\ge0\) or \(0\le t\le10\) must be applied at the end.

## 12.16 Misinterpreting model validity

If the model predicts \(\frac73\) seconds but the observation says about \(4\) seconds, the model may not be valid.

## 12.17 Trusting partially unclear screenshots too much

## Solution 1

\]

## Solution 2

Multiplying by \(x-3\) is unsafe because \(x-3\) changes sign. Use \((x-3)^2\):

\[
\frac{x+1}{x-3}<2
\]

\[
(x+1)(x-3)<2(x-3)^2
\]

\[
(x-3)[(x+1)-2(x-3)]<0
\]

\[
(x-3)(7-x)<0.
\]

Equivalent to \((x-3)(x-7)>0\), so

\[
\boxed{x<3\quad\text{or}\quad x>7.}
\]

## Solution 3

Multiply by \((x+2)^2>0\):

\[
x+2\ge(x+2)^2.
\]

Let \(u=x+2\):

\[
u\ge u^2\Rightarrow u(u-1)\le0.
\]

So \(0\le u\le1\), but \(u=0\) is excluded. Hence

\[
\boxed{-2<x\le-1.}
\]

\boxed{\{x:-1\le x\le2\}\cup\{x:x\ge4\}.}

Negative branch:

\[
-(x^2+2x-3)=x+3\Rightarrow -x^2-3x=0\Rightarrow x=0,-3.
\]

Valid boundaries are \(0\) and \(2\), so

\[
\boxed{0<x<2.}
\]

## Solution 14

We solve

\[
|4t-10|<|t^2-7t+10|,
\]

with \(0\le t\le10\).

Equality branch 1:

\[
4t-10=t^2-7t+10\Rightarrow t^2-11t+20=0\Rightarrow t=\frac{11\pm\sqrt{41}}2.
\]

Equality branch 2:

\[
4t-10=-(t^2-7t+10)\Rightarrow -t^2+3t=0\Rightarrow t=0,3.
\]

Testing intervals gives

\[
\boxed{\frac{11-\sqrt{41}}2<t<3\quad\text{or}\quad \frac{11+\sqrt{41}}2<t\le10.}
\]

Total duration:

\[
3-\frac{11-\sqrt{41}}2+10-\frac{11+\sqrt{41}}2=2.
\]

So the total duration is

\[
\boxed{2\text{ seconds}.}
\]

# 15. Exam Technique Notes

1. If the question says “use algebra”, show algebra.
2. Always write excluded values before multiplying.
3. Square denominators, but do not forget what they came from.
4. Factorise before expanding.
5. Sketches do not need perfect scale, but they need correct order.
6. Intersections are not the whole story in graphical inequalities.
7. Use exact forms in final answers.
8. Watch strict versus weak inequality signs.
9. Use modulus graphs to reject phantom roots.
10. For modelling questions, do not lose the domain.

## 19.2 Rational inequality checklist

- [ ] I identify denominator restrictions before solving.
- [ ] I avoid multiplying by unknown-sign expressions.
- [ ] I multiply safely by squared denominator factors.
- [ ] I cancel factors carefully.
- [ ] I bring everything to one side.
- [ ] I factorise before expanding where possible.
- [ ] I find critical values.
- [ ] I sketch the sign pattern.
- [ ] I remove excluded denominator values.

- [ ] I can find horizontal asymptotes using large-\(x\) behaviour.
- [ ] I can solve equations to find intersections.
- [ ] I can decide which graph is above or below.
- [ ] I remember that asymptotes may split solution intervals.

## 19.4 Modulus inequality checklist

- [ ] I can solve the positive branch equation.
- [ ] I can solve the negative branch equation.
- [ ] I can reject phantom roots using the graph.
- [ ] I keep exact surd values in final answers.
- [ ] I identify isolated solution points if graphs only touch.
