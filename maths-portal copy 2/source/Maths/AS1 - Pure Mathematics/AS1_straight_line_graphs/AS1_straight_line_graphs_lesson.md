## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Understand a line as all points satisfying an equation in \(x\) and/or \(y\).
2. Substitute coordinates into line equations to test whether points lie on a line.
3. Find \(x\)-intercepts and \(y\)-intercepts by setting \(y=0\) or \(x=0\).
4. Find and interpret the gradient of a straight line using \(m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\).
5. Use \(y=mx+c\) only when \(y\) is the subject.
6. Convert between \(y=mx+c\) and \(ax+by+c=0\).
7. Use \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\) to find equations of straight lines.
8. Find equations of lines from two points.
9. Find intersections of two lines by solving simultaneous equations.
10. Use gradient conditions for parallel and perpendicular lines.
11. Find distances between points and areas of triangles on coordinate axes.
12. Build, interpret and critique simple linear models.

## Big Picture Explanation

Straight-line graphs are a quiet workhorse of AS Pure Mathematics. They look familiar, but the A-Level leap is not the theory itself. The leap is the algebraic handling.

At this level, you are expected to move fluently between

\[
y=mx+c,
\]

\[
y-y_1=m(x-x_1),
\]

and

\[
ax+by+c=0,
\]

while also using line equations inside bigger problems involving intersections, lengths, areas, perpendicular gradients and real-life modelling.

## Key Definitions and Notation

### Line

A line consists of all points which satisfy some equation in terms of \(x\) and/or \(y\).

For example, the equation

\[
2x+y=5
\]

is a rule. A coordinate belongs to the line only if it makes the equation true.

### Satisfy an equation

A coordinate satisfies an equation if substituting its \(x\)- and \(y\)-values makes the equation true.

Test \((3,-1)\) in \(2x+y=5\):

\[
2(3)+(-1)=6-1=5.
\]

So \((3,-1)\) lies on the line.

Test \((3,2)\):

\[
2(3)+2=6+2=8.
\]

Since \(8\ne 5\), \((3,2)\) does not lie on the line.

### Gradient

The gradient is the steepness of a line. It tells us what \(y\) changes by as \(x\) increases by 1.

The symbol \(\Delta\) is the capital Greek letter delta and means "change in".

\[
m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}
\]

For two points \((x_1,y_1)\) and \((x_2,y_2)\),

\[
m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}.
\]

The order can be reversed only if both numerator and denominator are reversed consistently:

\[
m=\frac{y_1-y_2}{x_1-x_2}.
\]

### Collinear

Points are collinear if they lie on the same straight line.

### \(y=mx+c\)

In

\[
y=mx+c,
\]

\(m\) is the gradient and \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept.

This only works when the equation is genuinely in the form \(y=\cdots\). If the equation is \(2y=mx+c\), it is not yet in \(y=mx+c\) form.

### Standard form

Another common form of a straight line equation is

\[
ax+by+c=0,
\]

where \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are integers.

### Point-gradient form

The A-Level line-equation form is

\[
y-y_1=m(x-x_1).
\]

It is used when you know a gradient \(m\) and a point \((x_1,y_1)\) on the line.

## Core Theory

## 1. Vertical and horizontal lines

Consider a vertical line through \(x=2\). The equation is

\[
x=2.
\]

This means every point on the line has \(x\)-coordinate 2. For example,

\[
(2,4),\quad (2,1),\quad (2,-3)
\]

all satisfy \(x=2\).

A common mistake is to draw \(x=2\) as a horizontal line because the \(x\)-axis is horizontal. That is the trap. The equation \(x=2\) means the \(x\)-coordinate stays fixed at 2, so the line is vertical.

Similarly,

\[
y=2
\]

is horizontal because every point on the line has \(y\)-coordinate 2.

## 2. Substitution and intercepts

If a point lies on a line, its coordinates satisfy the equation of the line.

### Example 1: Find a missing coordinate

The point \((5,a)\) lies on the line

\[
y=3x+2.
\]

Since the point is \((5,a)\),

\[
x=5,\qquad y=a.
\]

Substitute into the equation:

\[
a=3(5)+2.
\]

\[
a=15+2.
\]

\[
a=17.
\]

### Example 2: Find where a line cuts the \(x\)-axis

Find the coordinate where

\[
2x+y=5
\]

cuts the \(x\)-axis.

On the \(x\)-axis,

\[
y=0.
\]

Substitute:

\[
2x+0=5.
\]

\[
2x=5.
\]

\[
x=\frac{5}{2}.
\]

Therefore the coordinate is

\[
\left(\frac{5}{2},0\right).
\]

In Pure Mathematics, prefer exact fractions. \(2.5\) is numerically correct, but \(\frac{5}{2}\) is the better exact form.

### Example 3: Find both intercepts

Determine where

\[
x+2y=3
\]

crosses the axes.

For the \(y\)-axis, \(x=0\):

\[
0+2y=3,
\]

\[
2y=3,
\]

\[
y=\frac{3}{2}.
\]

So the \(y\)-intercept is

\[
\left(0,\frac{3}{2}\right).
\]

For the \(x\)-axis, \(y=0\):

\[
x+2(0)=3,
\]

\[
x=3.
\]

So the \(x\)-intercept is

\[
(3,0).
\]

## 3. Gradient

The gradient is

\[
m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}.
\]

If \(y\) increases by 6 as \(x\) increases by 2, then for each increase of 1 in \(x\),

\[
y \text{ increases by } \frac{6}{2}=3.
\]

So the gradient is \(m=3\).

[VISUAL PLACEHOLDER: AS1StraightLineGraphsTikZ-001 | Source: Lesson PDF and transcript | Insert from tikz/AS1StraightLineGraphsTikZ-001.tex | Purpose: Show a general gradient triangle labelled \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\), and \(m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\).]

### Example 4: Gradient through two points

Find the gradient of the line through \((1,4)\) and \((3,10)\).

\[
m=\frac{10-4}{3-1}=\frac{6}{2}=3.
\]

### Example 5: Gradient with a negative result

Find the gradient of the line through \((5,7)\) and \((8,1)\).

\[
m=\frac{7-1}{5-8}=\frac{6}{-3}=-2.
\]

You could also reverse both subtractions:

\[
m=\frac{1-7}{8-5}=\frac{-6}{3}=-2.
\]

The order is allowed to reverse only because the numerator and denominator are both reversed.

### Example 6: Avoid a negative denominator

Find the gradient of the line through \((2,2)\) and \((-1,10)\).

\[
m=\frac{10-2}{-1-2}=\frac{8}{-3}=-\frac{8}{3}.
\]

Do not leave the answer as \(\frac{8}{-3}\). It looks unfinished.

---

## 4. Showing points are collinear

Show that the points

\[
A(3,4),\quad B(5,5),\quad C(11,8)
\]

all lie on a straight line.

Find the gradient between \(A\) and \(B\):

\[
m_{AB}=\frac{5-4}{5-3}=\frac{1}{2}.
\]

Find the gradient between \(B\) and \(C\):

\[
m_{BC}=\frac{8-5}{11-5}=\frac{3}{6}=\frac{1}{2}.
\]

Since

\[
m_{AB}=m_{BC},
\]

the gradients are the same. Therefore \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) are collinear.

Exam warning: do not stop after calculating the gradients. If the question says "show that", write the final conclusion sentence.

---

## 5. Missing coordinate using a gradient

The line joining \((2,-5)\) to \((4,a)\) has gradient \(-1\). Work out \(a\).

\[
\frac{a-(-5)}{4-2}=-1.
\]

\[
\frac{a+5}{2}=-1.
\]

Multiply both sides by 2:

\[
a+5=-2.
\]

Subtract 5:

\[
a=-7.
\]

## 6. The form \(y=mx+c\)

One form of a straight line is

\[
y=mx+c.
\]

Here \(m\) is the gradient and \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept.

Why does this work? The \(y\)-intercept is the value of \(y\) when \(x=0\). Substitute \(x=0\):

\[
y=m(0)+c=0+c=c.
\]

Also, if \(x\) increases by 1, compare

\[
y=mx+c
\]

with

\[
y=m(x+1)+c=mx+m+c.
\]

The new \(y\)-value is \(m\) more than before, so the gradient is \(m\).

### Example 7: Rearrange into \(y=mx+c\)

Determine the gradient and \(y\)-intercept of

\[
4x-3y+5=0.
\]

Move \(-3y\) to the other side:

\[
4x+5=3y.
\]

Write it as

\[
3y=4x+5.
\]

Divide every term by 3:

\[
y=\frac{4}{3}x+\frac{5}{3}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
m=\frac{4}{3},\qquad c=\frac{5}{3}.
\]

Warning: do not write \(y=\frac{4x+5}{3}\) if you are trying to identify \(m\) and \(c\). Split the terms.

---

## 7. Standard form \(ax+by+c=0\)

The standard form is

\[
ax+by+c=0,
\]

where \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are integers.

### Example 8: Convert to standard form

Express

\[
y=\frac{1}{3}x-\frac{2}{3}
\]

in the form \(ax+by+c=0\).

Clear the fractions by multiplying every term by 3:

\[
3y=x-2.
\]

Move everything to one side:

\[
x-3y-2=0.
\]

A valid answer is

\[
x-3y-2=0.
\]

You could also multiply every term by \(-1\):

\[
-x+3y+2=0.
\]

Both are equivalent.

### Example 9: Convert another equation to standard form

Express

\[
y=\frac{2}{5}x+\frac{3}{5}
\]

in the form \(ax+by+c=0\).

Multiply every term by 5:

\[
5y=2x+3.
\]

Move everything to one side:

\[
2x-5y+3=0.
\]

---

## 8. Deriving \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\)

Suppose \((x_1,y_1)\) is a fixed point on a line. Suppose \((x,y)\) is a general point on the same line.

The gradient between these two points is

\[
m=\frac{y-y_1}{x-x_1}.
\]

Multiply both sides by \(x-x_1\):

\[
m(x-x_1)=y-y_1.
\]

So

\[
y-y_1=m(x-x_1).
\]

Important notation:

- \(x_1\) and \(y_1\) are constants because they come from the known point.
- \(x\) and \(y\) are variables because they represent any point on the line.

### Example 10: Line through a point with known gradient

Find the equation of the line through \((3,5)\) with gradient 2.

Here,

\[
x_1=3,\qquad y_1=5,\qquad m=2.
\]

Substitute into point-gradient form:

\[
y-5=2(x-3).
\]

This is already an equation of the line.

If expanded:

\[
y-5=2x-6,
\]

\[
y=2x-1.
\]

## 9. Equation of a line through two points

If two points are given, first find the gradient. Then use \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\).

### Example 11: Two points to standard form

Find the equation of the line through \((4,5)\) and \((6,2)\), giving your answer in the form \(ax+by+c=0\).

First find the gradient:

\[
m=\frac{2-5}{6-4}=\frac{-3}{2}.
\]

Use point-gradient form with \((4,5)\):

\[
y-5=-\frac{3}{2}(x-4).
\]

Clear the fraction by multiplying by 2:

\[
2(y-5)=-3(x-4).
\]

Expand:

\[
2y-10=-3x+12.
\]

Move everything to one side:

\[
3x+2y-22=0.
\]

### Example 12: Another two-point equation

Find the equation of the line through \((-1,9)\) and \((4,5)\), giving your answer in the form \(ax+by+c=0\).

\[
m=\frac{5-9}{4-(-1)}=\frac{-4}{5}.
\]

Use \((-1,9)\):

\[
y-9=-\frac{4}{5}(x-(-1)).
\]

\[
y-9=-\frac{4}{5}(x+1).
\]

Multiply by 5:

\[
5(y-9)=-4(x+1).
\]

Expand:

\[
5y-45=-4x-4.
\]

Move everything to one side:

\[
4x+5y-41=0.
\]

## 10. Intersections of lines

To find where two lines intersect, solve their equations simultaneously. At the point of intersection, both lines have the same \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates.

### Example 13: Intersection of two lines

The lines

\[
y=3x
\]

and

\[
x+2y=4
\]

intersect at \(P\). Find \(P\).

Substitute \(y=3x\) into \(x+2y=4\):

\[
x+2(3x)=4.
\]

\[
x+6x=4.
\]

\[
7x=4.
\]

\[
x=\frac{4}{7}.
\]

Now find \(y\):

\[
y=3\left(\frac{4}{7}\right)=\frac{12}{7}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
P=\left(\frac{4}{7},\frac{12}{7}\right).
\]

### Example 14: Axis intercept and area

The line \(x+2y=4\) intersects the \(x\)-axis at \(Q\). Find \(Q\), then find the area of triangle \(OPQ\), where \(O=(0,0)\).

On the \(x\)-axis, \(y=0\):

\[
x+2(0)=4,
\]

\[
x=4.
\]

So \(Q=(4,0)\).

The base \(OQ\) has length 4. The height of the triangle is the \(y\)-coordinate of \(P\): \(\frac{12}{7}\).

\[
\text{Area}=\frac12 \times 4 \times \frac{12}{7}=2\times \frac{12}{7}=\frac{24}{7}.
\]

So the area is

\[
\frac{24}{7}\text{ units}^2.
\]

## 11. Parallel and perpendicular lines

Parallel lines have the same gradient.

\[
m_1=m_2
\]

Perpendicular lines have gradients that are negative reciprocals. Another test is

\[
m_1m_2=-1.
\]

| Gradient \(m\) | Gradient of perpendicular line |
|---|---|
| \(2\) | \(-\frac12\) |
| \(-3\) | \(\frac13\) |
| \(\frac14\) | \(-4\) |
| \(5\) | \(-\frac15\) |
| \(-\frac27\) | \(\frac72\) |
| \(\frac75\) | \(-\frac57\) |

[VISUAL PLACEHOLDER: AS1StraightLineGraphsSVG-003 | Source: Lesson PDF and transcript | Insert from svg/AS1StraightLineGraphsSVG-003.svg | Purpose: Show parallel lines with equal gradients and perpendicular lines with negative reciprocal gradients.]

### Example 15: Perpendicular line through a point

A line goes through \((9,10)\) and is perpendicular to \(y=3x+2\). Find its equation.

The gradient of \(y=3x+2\) is 3. The perpendicular gradient is

\[
-\frac13.
\]

Use point-gradient form:

\[
y-10=-\frac13(x-9).
\]

If expanded:

\[
y-10=-\frac13x+3,
\]

\[
y=-\frac13x+13.
\]

### Example 16: Multi-step perpendicular problem

Line \(L_1\) goes through \(A(1,3)\) and \(B(3,-1)\). Line \(L_2\) is perpendicular to \(L_1\) and passes through \(B\). Where does \(L_2\) cross the \(x\)-axis?

\[
m_{AB}=\frac{-1-3}{3-1}=\frac{-4}{2}=-2.
\]

The perpendicular gradient is

\[
\frac12.
\]

Use point-gradient form through \(B(3,-1)\):

\[
y-(-1)=\frac12(x-3).
\]

\[
y+1=\frac12(x-3).
\]

At the \(x\)-axis, \(y=0\):

\[
0+1=\frac12(x-3).
\]

\[
1=\frac12(x-3).
\]

\[
2=x-3.
\]

\[
x=5.
\]

So \(L_2\) crosses the \(x\)-axis at \((5,0)\).

## 12. Distance between two points

The distance between two points comes from Pythagoras. If the changes in \(x\) and \(y\) are \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\), then

\[
d=\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}.
\]

Unlike gradient, the order does not matter for distance, because the differences are squared.

### Example 17: Distance between \((1,6)\) and \((5,9)\)

The change in \(x\) is \(5-1=4\). The change in \(y\) is \(9-6=3\).

\[
d=\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=\sqrt{16+9}=\sqrt{25}=5.
\]

### Example 18: Surd distance

If the change in \(x\) is 2 and the change in \(y\) is 3, then

\[
d=\sqrt{2^2+3^2}=\sqrt{4+9}=\sqrt{13}.
\]

Do not decimalise unless the context asks for an approximation.

## 13. Length and area problems

Coordinate geometry questions often combine simultaneous equations, intercepts, distance formula, and triangle area.

### Example 19: Length and area using two lines

The two lines are

\[
2y=x+4
\]

and

\[
x+y=8.
\]

They intersect at \(P\). The first line crosses the \(y\)-axis at \(Q\). The second line crosses the \(y\)-axis at \(R\). Find \(P\), \(Q\), \(PQ\), and the area of triangle \(PQR\).

#### Step 1: Find \(P\)

From \(x+y=8\),

\[
y=8-x.
\]

Substitute into \(2y=x+4\):

\[
2(8-x)=x+4.
\]

\[
16-2x=x+4.
\]

\[
16=3x+4.
\]

\[
12=3x.
\]

\[
x=4.
\]

Then

\[
y=8-4=4.
\]

So \(P=(4,4)\).

#### Step 2: Find \(Q\)

For \(2y=x+4\), on the \(y\)-axis \(x=0\):

\[
2y=0+4=4,
\]

\[
y=2.
\]

So \(Q=(0,2)\).

#### Step 3: Find \(PQ\)

\[
PQ=\sqrt{(4-0)^2+(4-2)^2}=\sqrt{4^2+2^2}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5.
\]

#### Step 4: Find \(R\)

For \(x+y=8\), on the \(y\)-axis \(x=0\):

\[
y=8.
\]

So \(R=(0,8)\).

#### Step 5: Find the area of triangle \(PQR\)

The segment \(QR\) is vertical:

\[
QR=8-2=6.
\]

The perpendicular distance from \(P=(4,4)\) to the \(y\)-axis is 4.

\[
\text{Area}=\frac12 \times 6 \times 4=12.
\]

Therefore,

\[
\text{Area of }PQR=12\text{ units}^2.
\]

## 14. Optional extension: shortest distance from the origin to a line

Find the shortest distance from the origin to

\[
3x+4y=25.
\]

First find the gradient of the line:

\[
4y=25-3x,
\]

\[
y=-\frac34x+\frac{25}{4}.
\]

The gradient is \(-\frac34\). The shortest distance from a point to a line is perpendicular to the line, so the perpendicular gradient is \(\frac43\).

The perpendicular line through the origin is

\[
y=\frac43x.
\]

Solve with \(3x+4y=25\):

\[
3x+4\left(\frac43x\right)=25.
\]

\[
3x+\frac{16}{3}x=25.
\]

\[
\frac{9}{3}x+\frac{16}{3}x=25.
\]

\[
\frac{25}{3}x=25.
\]

\[
25x=75.
\]

\[
x=3.
\]

Then

\[
y=\frac43(3)=4.
\]

The closest point is \((3,4)\). The distance from the origin is

\[
\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=5.
\]

## 15. Linear modelling

A mathematical model is an attempt to represent a real-life situation using mathematical concepts.

A linear relationship means there is a fixed increase or decrease in one variable each time the other variable goes up by 1 unit.

A linear model often has the form

\[
y=mx+c,
\]

where \(x\) is the input variable, \(y\) is the output variable, \(m\) is the rate of change, and \(c\) is the value predicted when \(x=0\).

### Interpolation and extrapolation

Interpolation means making a prediction inside the range of the data.

Extrapolation means making a prediction outside the range of the data.

- "inter" means inside;
- "extra" means outside.

### Example 20: Temperature and altitude

The temperature \(y\), in degrees Fahrenheit, is modelled at altitude \(x\), in metres. Assume the line goes through \((0,70)\) and \((250,20)\).

Since \((0,70)\) is the \(y\)-intercept,

\[
c=70.
\]

Find the gradient:

\[
m=\frac{20-70}{250-0}=\frac{-50}{250}=-\frac15=-0.2.
\]

So the model is

\[
y=-0.2x+70.
\]

If \(y=0\),

\[
0=-0.2x+70.
\]

\[
0.2x=70.
\]

\[
x=350.
\]

The model predicts \(0^\circ\text{F}\) at 350 metres. If the data range was only up to 250 metres, this is extrapolation and is less reliable.

### Example 21: Leaking tank

A tank contains water and starts to leak. After 24 minutes, the volume is \(4\text{ m}^3\). After 60 minutes, the volume is \(2.8\text{ m}^3\).

The points are

\[
(24,4),\qquad (60,2.8).
\]

Find the gradient:

\[
m=\frac{2.8-4}{60-24}=\frac{-1.2}{36}.
\]

Since \(1.2=\frac65\),

\[
m=\frac{-\frac65}{36}=-\frac65\times\frac{1}{36}=-\frac{6}{180}=-\frac{1}{30}.
\]

Use point-gradient form with \((24,4)\):

\[
V-4=-\frac{1}{30}(t-24).
\]

Expand:

\[
V-4=-\frac{1}{30}t+\frac{24}{30}.
\]

\[
V-4=-\frac{1}{30}t+\frac45.
\]

Add 4:

\[
V=-\frac{1}{30}t+\frac45+4.
\]

\[
V=-\frac{1}{30}t+\frac45+\frac{20}{5}.
\]

\[
V=-\frac{1}{30}t+\frac{24}{5}.
\]

Initial volume means \(t=0\):

\[
V=\frac{24}{5}=4.8.
\]

Empty means \(V=0\):

\[
0=-\frac{1}{30}t+\frac{24}{5}.
\]

\[
\frac{1}{30}t=\frac{24}{5}.
\]

\[
t=30\times\frac{24}{5}=6\times24=144.
\]

The tank empties after 144 minutes.

The model assumes the water leaks at a constant rate. This may not be suitable because as the water level changes, the pressure may change, so the leak rate may not remain constant.

### Example 22: Plant growth model

A plant has height \(h\) metres \(t\) years after it was planted. After 2 years, its height is 1.43 m. After 5 years, its height is 3.23 m.

The points are

\[
(2,1.43),\qquad (5,3.23).
\]

\[
m=\frac{3.23-1.43}{5-2}=\frac{1.80}{3}=0.6.
\]

Use \((2,1.43)\):

\[
h-1.43=0.6(t-2).
\]

\[
h-1.43=0.6t-1.2.
\]

\[
h=0.6t-1.2+1.43.
\]

\[
h=0.6t+0.23.
\]

When planted, \(t=0\):

\[
h=0.6(0)+0.23=0.23\text{ m}=23\text{ cm}.
\]

If the actual height was approximately 55 cm, this does not support the use of the linear model from planting onward.

## Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

### Trap 1: Drawing \(x=2\) horizontally

\(x=2\) is vertical. \(y=2\) is horizontal.

### Trap 2: Mixing up intercepts

For an \(x\)-intercept, set \(y=0\). For a \(y\)-intercept, set \(x=0\).

### Trap 3: Reversing only one part of the gradient calculation

This is wrong:

\[
m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_1-x_2}.
\]

The order must match:

\[
m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}
\]

or

\[
m=\frac{y_1-y_2}{x_1-x_2}.
\]

### Trap 4: Forgetting double negatives

If the change is from \(-5\) to \(a\), then

\[
a-(-5)=a+5.
\]

### Trap 5: Using \(y=mx+c\) when \(y\) is not the subject

If \(4x-3y+5=0\), you cannot immediately read off the gradient. First rearrange into \(y=mx+c\).

### Trap 6: Decimalising exact answers in Pure Maths

Use \(\frac{5}{2}\) rather than \(2.5\), unless a decimal approximation is requested.

### Trap 7: Not clearing fractions in standard form

For \(ax+by+c=0\), \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) should be integers.

### Trap 8: Forgetting the final conclusion in a "show that" question

After proving gradients are equal, write a final conclusion, for example:

\[
\therefore A, B, C\text{ are collinear.}
\]

### Trap 9: Using non-perpendicular base and height

For triangle area, base and height must be perpendicular.

### Trap 10: Treating a model as truth

A linear model predicts. It does not prove what must happen in reality.

Use phrases such as "According to the model" or "The model assumes".

## Exam Technique Notes

1. If you need a line equation, ask: do I have a gradient and a point?
2. If yes, use \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\).
3. If you have two points, find the gradient first.
4. If a question asks for standard form, clear fractions and move everything to one side.
5. If lines intersect, solve simultaneously.
6. If a line is perpendicular, take the negative reciprocal of the gradient.
7. If finding an axis intercept, substitute \(x=0\) or \(y=0\) directly.
8. If a context says "linear model", think straight-line equation even if the variables are not \(x\) and \(y\).
9. Use exact fractions in pure questions.
10. Use decimals in modelling only when they help the real-world interpretation.
