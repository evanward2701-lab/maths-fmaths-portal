## Big Picture Explanation

Algebraic expressions are the toolkit shelf for AS Pure Mathematics. Index laws help you control powers. Expanding and factorising let you move between different shapes of the same expression. Surds and rationalising denominators keep exact answers neat instead of turning them into calculator fog.

This lesson is not just about getting answers. It is about learning when two expressions are genuinely equivalent, and when a shortcut has quietly smuggled in an illegal step.

---

## Key Definitions and Notation

### Base, exponent, index and power

In an expression such as

\[
3^5,
\]

the number \(3\) is the **base**.

The number \(5\) is the **exponent**, **index** or **power**.

The plural of **index** is **indices**.

### Coefficient

The **coefficient** of a term is the constant multiplier in front of it.

For example, in

\[
4x^2,
\]

the coefficient is \(4\).

### Linear expression

A **linear expression** is of the form

\[
ax+b.
\]

It is called linear because its graph would form a straight line.

### Factorised expression

An expression is **factorised** when it is written as a product of expressions.

For example,

\[
x(x+1)(x+2)
\]

is factorised because the outer operation is multiplication.

But

\[
x(x+1)+(x-1)(x+1)
\]

is not fully factorised because the outer operation is addition.

### Surd

A **surd** is a root of a number that does not simplify to a rational number.

For example,

\[
\sqrt{2}
\]

is a surd, but

\[
\sqrt{16}=4
\]

is not a surd.

### Rational number

A **rational number** is any number that can be written in the form

\[
\frac{a}{b},
\]

where \(a\) and \(b\) are integers and \(b\ne0\).

For example,

\[
\frac23,\qquad 4=\frac41
\]

are rational numbers.

But

\[
\pi,\qquad \sqrt2
\]

are not rational numbers.

### Rationalising the denominator

To **rationalise the denominator** means to rewrite a fraction so that the denominator is rational.

For example,

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2}
\]

has an irrational denominator, but

\[
\frac{\sqrt2}{2}
\]

has a rational denominator.

### Conjugate

For a denominator such as

\[
\sqrt2+1,
\]

the **conjugate** is

\[
\sqrt2-1.
\]

It is the same expression with the sign in the middle reversed.

## Core Theory

## 1. Index Laws

For suitable non-zero bases where required:

\[
a^m\times a^n=a^{m+n}
\]

\[
\frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}
\]

\[
(a^m)^n=a^{mn}
\]

\[
(ab)^n=a^n b^n
\]

\[
a^0=1\qquad (a\ne0)
\]

\[
a^{-m}=\frac1{a^m}
\]

\[
a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}
\]

\[
a^{m/n}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m
\]

### Important condition

The multiplication and division laws require the **same base**.

For example,

\[
x^3\times x^2=x^5
\]

but

\[
2^x\times 3^x
\]

does not use the same-base addition law. Instead, because both powers are the same, we use the product power law backwards:

\[
2^x\times 3^x=(2\times3)^x=6^x.
\]

---

## 2. Worked Index Examples

### Example 1: Simplify \((a^3)^2\times 2a^2\)

\[
(a^3)^2\times 2a^2
\]

First use the power of a power rule:

\[
(a^3)^2=a^{3\times2}=a^6.
\]

So

\[
(a^3)^2\times 2a^2=a^6\times 2a^2.
\]

The bases are the same, so add the indices:

\[
a^6\times a^2=a^{6+2}=a^8.
\]

The coefficient \(2\) stays in front:

\[
a^6\times 2a^2=2a^8.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{2a^8}.
\]

---

### Example 2: Simplify \((4x^3y)^3\)

\[
(4x^3y)^3
\]

Every factor inside the bracket is cubed:

\[
(4x^3y)^3=4^3(x^3)^3y^3.
\]

Now simplify each part:

\[
4^3=64
\]

\[
(x^3)^3=x^{3\times3}=x^9
\]

\[
y^3=y^3.
\]

Therefore

\[
(4x^3y)^3=64x^9y^3.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{64x^9y^3}.
\]

---

### Example 3: Simplify \(2x^2(3+5x)-x(4-x^2)\)

\[
2x^2(3+5x)-x(4-x^2)
\]

Expand the first bracket:

\[
2x^2(3+5x)=2x^2\cdot3+2x^2\cdot5x
\]

\[
=6x^2+10x^3.
\]

Now expand the second part carefully:

\[
-x(4-x^2)
\]

This means multiply the bracket by \(-x\), not by \(x\).

\[
-x(4-x^2)=(-x)\cdot4+(-x)\cdot(-x^2)
\]

\[
=-4x+x^3.
\]

So the full expression is

\[
2x^2(3+5x)-x(4-x^2)
=6x^2+10x^3-4x+x^3.
\]

Collect like terms:

\[
10x^3+x^3=11x^3.
\]

Therefore

\[
6x^2+10x^3-4x+x^3
=11x^3+6x^2-4x.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{11x^3+6x^2-4x}.
\]

### Warning

Do not change powers when collecting like terms.

\[
10x^3+x^3=11x^3,
\]

not \(9x^3\), not \(10x^4\), and not \(11x^6\).

Adding like terms changes the coefficient only.

---

### Example 4: Simplify \(\dfrac{x^3-2x}{3x^2}\)

\[
\frac{x^3-2x}{3x^2}
\]

Because the numerator is a sum/difference over a single common denominator, we can split it:

\[
\frac{x^3-2x}{3x^2}
=
\frac{x^3}{3x^2}-\frac{2x}{3x^2}.
\]

Simplify the first fraction:

\[
\frac{x^3}{3x^2}
=
\frac13 x^{3-2}
=
\frac13x.
\]

Simplify the second fraction:

\[
\frac{2x}{3x^2}
=
\frac23 x^{1-2}
=
\frac23x^{-1}.
\]

So

\[
\frac{x^3-2x}{3x^2}
=
\frac13x-\frac23x^{-1}.
\]

Since

\[
x^{-1}=\frac1x,
\]

we can also write

\[
\frac13x-\frac23x^{-1}
=
\frac{x}{3}-\frac{2}{3x}.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{\frac13x-\frac23x^{-1}}
\]

or

\[
\boxed{\frac{x}{3}-\frac{2}{3x}}.
\]

### Warning

You may split

\[
\frac{a+b}{c}=\frac ac+\frac bc,
\]

but you may not split

\[
\frac{a}{b+c}
\]

as

\[
\frac ab+\frac ac.
\]

That is an algebra trapdoor.

---

### Example 5: Simplify \(\left(\dfrac{2a^5}{a^2}\right)^2\times3a\)

\[
\left(\frac{2a^5}{a^2}\right)^2\times3a
\]

First simplify inside the bracket:

\[
\frac{2a^5}{a^2}=2a^{5-2}=2a^3.
\]

So

\[
\left(\frac{2a^5}{a^2}\right)^2\times3a
=
(2a^3)^2\times3a.
\]

Square both parts inside the bracket:

\[
(2a^3)^2=2^2(a^3)^2=4a^6.
\]

Therefore

\[
(2a^3)^2\times3a
=
4a^6\times3a.
\]

The \(a\) has an invisible power of \(1\):

\[
a=a^1.
\]

So

\[
4a^6\times3a
=
12a^{6+1}
=
12a^7.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{12a^7}.
\]

### Warning

Every part inside the bracket must be squared. The \(2\) becomes \(4\).

---

### Example 6: Simplify \(\dfrac{2x+x^5}{4x^3}\)

\[
\frac{2x+x^5}{4x^3}
\]

Split over the common denominator:

\[
\frac{2x+x^5}{4x^3}
=
\frac{2x}{4x^3}+\frac{x^5}{4x^3}.
\]

Simplify the first fraction:

\[
\frac{2x}{4x^3}
=
\frac12 x^{1-3}
=
\frac12x^{-2}.
\]

Simplify the second fraction:

\[
\frac{x^5}{4x^3}
=
\frac14 x^{5-3}
=
\frac14x^2.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{2x+x^5}{4x^3}
=
\frac12x^{-2}+\frac14x^2.
\]

Using \(x^{-2}=\dfrac1{x^2}\),

\[
\frac12x^{-2}+\frac14x^2
=
\frac{1}{2x^2}+\frac{x^2}{4}.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{\frac12x^{-2}+\frac14x^2}
\]

or

\[
\boxed{\frac{1}{2x^2}+\frac{x^2}{4}}.
\]

## 3. Expanding Brackets

The evidence warns against relying on “FOIL” as a rule. Instead, use the more general method:

> Multiply each term in the first bracket by each term in the second bracket.

This works for two brackets, three brackets and beyond.

### Example 7: Expand \((x-y)(x+y-1)\)

\[
(x-y)(x+y-1)
\]

Multiply \(x\) by every term in the second bracket:

\[
x(x+y-1)=x^2+xy-x.
\]

Multiply \(-y\) by every term in the second bracket:

\[
-y(x+y-1)=-xy-y^2+y.
\]

Now combine:

\[
(x-y)(x+y-1)
=
x^2+xy-x-xy-y^2+y.
\]

The \(xy\) terms cancel:

\[
xy-xy=0.
\]

So

\[
x^2+xy-x-xy-y^2+y
=
x^2-y^2-x+y.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{x^2-y^2-x+y}.
\]

---

### Example 8: Expand \((x+1)(x+2)(x+3)\)

First multiply two brackets:

\[
(x+2)(x+3)
=
x^2+3x+2x+6
=
x^2+5x+6.
\]

Now multiply by the remaining bracket:

\[
(x+1)(x^2+5x+6).
\]

Multiply \(x\) by each term:

\[
x(x^2+5x+6)=x^3+5x^2+6x.
\]

Multiply \(1\) by each term:

\[
1(x^2+5x+6)=x^2+5x+6.
\]

Now add:

\[
x^3+5x^2+6x+x^2+5x+6.
\]

Collect like terms:

\[
5x^2+x^2=6x^2
\]

\[
6x+5x=11x.
\]

So

\[
(x+1)(x+2)(x+3)
=
x^3+6x^2+11x+6.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{x^3+6x^2+11x+6}.
\]

---

### Example 9: Expand \((x+5)(x-2)(x+1)\)

First multiply two brackets:

\[
(x-2)(x+1)
=
x^2+x-2x-2
=
x^2-x-2.
\]

Now multiply by \((x+5)\):

\[
(x+5)(x^2-x-2).
\]

Multiply \(x\) by each term:

\[
x(x^2-x-2)=x^3-x^2-2x.
\]

Multiply \(5\) by each term:

\[
5(x^2-x-2)=5x^2-5x-10.
\]

Now add:

\[
x^3-x^2-2x+5x^2-5x-10.
\]

Collect like terms:

\[
-x^2+5x^2=4x^2
\]

\[
-2x-5x=-7x.
\]

Therefore

\[
(x+5)(x-2)(x+1)
=
x^3+4x^2-7x-10.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{x^3+4x^2-7x-10}.
\]

---

### Example 10: Expand \(2(x-3)(x-4)\)

First multiply the brackets:

\[
(x-3)(x-4)
=
x^2-4x-3x+12
=
x^2-7x+12.
\]

Now multiply by \(2\):

\[
2(x^2-7x+12)
=
2x^2-14x+24.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{2x^2-14x+24}.
\]

---

### Example 11: Expand \((2x-1)^3\)

Write the cube as three repeated brackets:

\[
(2x-1)^3=(2x-1)(2x-1)(2x-1).
\]

First multiply two brackets:

\[
(2x-1)(2x-1)
=
4x^2-2x-2x+1
=
4x^2-4x+1.
\]

Now multiply by the third bracket:

\[
(2x-1)(4x^2-4x+1).
\]

Multiply \(2x\) by each term:

\[
2x(4x^2-4x+1)=8x^3-8x^2+2x.
\]

Multiply \(-1\) by each term:

\[
-1(4x^2-4x+1)=-4x^2+4x-1.
\]

Now add:

\[
8x^3-8x^2+2x-4x^2+4x-1.
\]

Collect like terms:

\[
-8x^2-4x^2=-12x^2
\]

\[
2x+4x=6x.
\]

Therefore

\[
(2x-1)^3
=
8x^3-12x^2+6x-1.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{8x^3-12x^2+6x-1}.
\]

## 4. Factorising

Factorising is the opposite of expanding brackets.

More formally, a factorised expression is written as a product of expressions.

### Always look for a common factor first

This is one of the most useful habits in this chapter.

For example,

\[
x^3+2x^2
\]

has a common factor of \(x^2\):

\[
x^3+2x^2=x^2(x+2).
\]

Also,

\[
4x-8
\]

has a common factor of \(4\):

\[
4x-8=4(x-2).
\]

---

## 5. Factorising Quadratics

### Example 12: Factorise \(x^2-5x-14\)

We need two numbers that:

- add to \(-5\)
- multiply to \(-14\)

The numbers are \(-7\) and \(2\), because

\[
-7+2=-5
\]

and

\[
-7\times2=-14.
\]

Therefore

\[
x^2-5x-14=(x-7)(x+2).
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{(x-7)(x+2)}.
\]

---

### Example 13: Factorise \(2x^2+5x-12\)

When the coefficient of \(x^2\) is not \(1\), one method is to split the middle term.

For

\[
2x^2+5x-12,
\]

multiply the first coefficient by the constant:

\[
2\times(-12)=-24.
\]

We need two numbers that:

- add to \(5\)
- multiply to \(-24\)

The numbers are \(8\) and \(-3\), since

\[
8+(-3)=5
\]

and

\[
8\times(-3)=-24.
\]

Split the middle term:

\[
2x^2+5x-12
=
2x^2+8x-3x-12.
\]

Factorise the first pair:

\[
2x^2+8x=2x(x+4).
\]

Factorise the second pair:

\[
-3x-12=-3(x+4).
\]

So

\[
2x^2+8x-3x-12
=
2x(x+4)-3(x+4).
\]

Now factor out the repeated bracket:

\[
2x(x+4)-3(x+4)
=
(x+4)(2x-3).
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{(x+4)(2x-3)}.
\]

---

### Example 14: Factorise \(6x^2+x-2\)

For

\[
6x^2+x-2,
\]

multiply the first coefficient by the constant:

\[
6\times(-2)=-12.
\]

We need two numbers that:

- add to \(1\)
- multiply to \(-12\)

The numbers are \(4\) and \(-3\), since

\[
4+(-3)=1
\]

and

\[
4\times(-3)=-12.
\]

Split the middle term:

\[
6x^2+x-2
=
6x^2+4x-3x-2.
\]

Factorise the first pair:

\[
6x^2+4x=2x(3x+2).
\]

Factorise the second pair:

\[
-3x-2=-1(3x+2).
\]

So

\[
6x^2+4x-3x-2
=
2x(3x+2)-1(3x+2).
\]

Factor out the repeated bracket:

\[
2x(3x+2)-1(3x+2)
=
(3x+2)(2x-1).
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{(3x+2)(2x-1)}.
\]

---

## 6. Factorising Cubic and Higher-Power Expressions

### Example 15: Factorise \(x^3-7x^2+12x\)

First look for a common factor.

Every term contains \(x\), so factor out \(x\):

\[
x^3-7x^2+12x
=
x(x^2-7x+12).
\]

Now factorise the quadratic.

We need two numbers that:

- add to \(-7\)
- multiply to \(12\)

The numbers are \(-3\) and \(-4\), because

\[
-3+(-4)=-7
\]

and

\[
(-3)(-4)=12.
\]

So

\[
x^2-7x+12=(x-3)(x-4).
\]

Therefore

\[
x^3-7x^2+12x
=
x(x-3)(x-4).
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{x(x-3)(x-4)}.
\]

---

### Example 16: Factorise \(x^4-1\)

Recognise a difference of two squares:

\[
x^4-1=(x^2)^2-1^2.
\]

So

\[
x^4-1=(x^2-1)(x^2+1).
\]

Now \(x^2-1\) is also a difference of two squares:

\[
x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1).
\]

Therefore

\[
x^4-1=(x^2+1)(x-1)(x+1).
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{(x^2+1)(x-1)(x+1)}.
\]

---

### Enrichment Note: Difference of Cubes

The evidence mentions:

\[
x^3-1=(x-1)(x^2+x+1).
\]

More generally,

\[
x^3-y^3=(x-y)(x^2+xy+y^2).
\]

This is useful enrichment, especially for more advanced problem solving, but it is not treated as required core content for this lesson.

## 7. Negative and Fractional Indices

### Zero powers

For \(a\ne0\),

\[
a^0=1.
\]

For example,

\[
3^3=27,
\]

\[
3^2=9,
\]

\[
3^1=3.
\]

Each time the power decreases by \(1\), the value is divided by \(3\).

So

\[
3^0=\frac{3^1}{3}=\frac33=1.
\]

Then

\[
3^{-1}=\frac{3^0}{3}=\frac13,
\]

and

\[
3^{-2}=\frac{3^{-1}}{3}=\frac13\div3=\frac19.
\]

This is consistent with

\[
3^{-2}=\frac1{3^2}=\frac19.
\]

### Note on \(0^0\)

The evidence explains that \(0^0\) creates a clash between two patterns:

\[
0^n=0
\]

for positive \(n\), but

\[
a^0=1
\]

for non-zero \(a\). So \(0^0\) is not assigned a value in this lesson.

---

### Fractional powers

\[
a^{1/2}=\sqrt{a}
\]

\[
a^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{a}
\]

\[
a^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{a}\right)^2.
\]

The denominator of the fraction tells you the root.

The numerator tells you the power.

So

\[
a^{2/3}
=
\left(a^{1/3}\right)^2
=
\left(\sqrt[3]{a}\right)^2.
\]

---

### Proof idea: \(x^{1/2}=\sqrt{x}\)

We know

\[
\sqrt{x}\times\sqrt{x}=x.
\]

We also know

\[
x^{1/2}\times x^{1/2}=x^{1/2+1/2}=x^1=x.
\]

Both \(\sqrt{x}\) and \(x^{1/2}\) are quantities which multiply by themselves to give \(x\).

So

\[
x^{1/2}=\sqrt{x}.
\]

For the same reason,

\[
x^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{x}.
\]

---

### Positive square root warning

\[
\sqrt9=3,
\]

not \(\pm3\).

The square root symbol means the positive square root. The \(\pm\) only appears when solving an equation such as

\[
x^2=9.
\]

Then

\[
x=\pm3.
\]

---

## 8. Worked Examples with Negative and Fractional Indices

### Example 17: Evaluate \(27^{-1/3}\)

\[
27^{-1/3}
\]

A negative power means reciprocal:

\[
27^{-1/3}=\frac{1}{27^{1/3}}.
\]

Now

\[
27^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{27}=3.
\]

Therefore

\[
27^{-1/3}=\frac13.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{\frac13}.
\]

---

### Example 18: Evaluate \(32^{2/5}\)

\[
32^{2/5}
\]

Split the fractional power:

\[
32^{2/5}=\left(32^{1/5}\right)^2.
\]

Now \(32^{1/5}\) means the fifth root of \(32\).

Since

\[
2\times2\times2\times2\times2=32,
\]

we have

\[
32^{1/5}=2.
\]

So

\[
32^{2/5}=2^2=4.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{4}.
\]

### Warning

Do not divide by \(5\). The expression

\[
32^{1/5}
\]

means the fifth root of \(32\), not \(32\div5\).

---

### Example 19: Simplify \(\left(\dfrac19x^6y\right)^{1/2}\)

\[
\left(\frac19x^6y\right)^{1/2}
\]

Apply the power of \(\frac12\) to each factor:

\[
\left(\frac19x^6y\right)^{1/2}
=
\left(\frac19\right)^{1/2}(x^6)^{1/2}y^{1/2}.
\]

Now

\[
\left(\frac19\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\frac19}=\frac13.
\]

Also,

\[
(x^6)^{1/2}=x^{6\times1/2}=x^3.
\]

So

\[
\left(\frac19x^6y\right)^{1/2}
=
\frac13x^3y^{1/2}.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{\frac13x^3y^{1/2}}.
\]

This can also be written as

\[
\boxed{\frac{x^3\sqrt{y}}{3}}.
\]

---

### Example 20: Evaluate \(\left(\dfrac{27}{8}\right)^{-2/3}\)

\[
\left(\frac{27}{8}\right)^{-2/3}
\]

Deal with the negative power by taking the reciprocal:

\[
\left(\frac{27}{8}\right)^{-2/3}
=
\left(\frac{8}{27}\right)^{2/3}.
\]

Now split the power \(2/3\):

\[
\left(\frac{8}{27}\right)^{2/3}
=
\left[\left(\frac{8}{27}\right)^{1/3}\right]^2.
\]

Take the cube root of the numerator and denominator:

\[
\left(\frac{8}{27}\right)^{1/3}
=
\frac{\sqrt[3]{8}}{\sqrt[3]{27}}
=
\frac23.
\]

Now square:

\[
\left(\frac23\right)^2=\frac49.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{\frac49}.
\]

---

### Example 21: If \(b=\dfrac19a^2\), determine \(3b^{-2}\) in the form \(ka^n\)

Given

\[
b=\frac19a^2.
\]

We want

\[
3b^{-2}.
\]

Substitute the expression for \(b\):

\[
3b^{-2}
=
3\left(\frac19a^2\right)^{-2}.
\]

Apply the power \(-2\) to each factor:

\[
3\left(\frac19a^2\right)^{-2}
=
3\left(\frac19\right)^{-2}(a^2)^{-2}.
\]

Now

\[
\left(\frac19\right)^{-2}=9^2=81.
\]

Also,

\[
(a^2)^{-2}=a^{2\times(-2)}=a^{-4}.
\]

Therefore

\[
3\left(\frac19a^2\right)^{-2}
=
3(81)a^{-4}.
\]

\[
3(81)=243.
\]

So

\[
3b^{-2}=243a^{-4}.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{243a^{-4}}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{k=243,\qquad n=-4}.
\]

---

### Example 22: If \(9\sqrt3=3^n\), find \(n\)

Write everything as powers of \(3\):

\[
9\sqrt3=3^2\times3^{1/2}.
\]

Use the same-base multiplication law:

\[
3^2\times3^{1/2}=3^{2+1/2}.
\]

\[
2+\frac12=\frac52.
\]

So

\[
9\sqrt3=3^{5/2}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{n=\frac52}.
\]

### Notation warning

Use

\[
\frac52
\]

rather than the mixed number

\[
2\frac12.
\]

Algebra prefers improper fractions because they behave more cleanly inside powers.

## 9. Surds

### Surd Laws

For suitable non-negative values:

\[
\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}=\sqrt{ab}
\]

\[
\sqrt{\frac ab}=\frac{\sqrt a}{\sqrt b}
\]

where \(b>0\).

These laws work in both directions.

---

### Example 23: Simplify \(2\sqrt3\)

This is already in simplified surd form:

\[
\boxed{2\sqrt3}.
\]

We write the rational multiplier first. We write \(2\sqrt3\), not \(\sqrt3\,2\), because the second form can be visually confusing.

---

### Example 24: Simplify \(3\sqrt5\times2\sqrt5\)

\[
3\sqrt5\times2\sqrt5
\]

Multiply the rational coefficients:

\[
3\times2=6.
\]

Multiply the surds:

\[
\sqrt5\times\sqrt5=5.
\]

So

\[
3\sqrt5\times2\sqrt5
=
6\times5
=
30.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{30}.
\]

---

### Example 25: Simplify \(\sqrt8\)

\[
\sqrt8
\]

Write \(8\) as \(4\times2\):

\[
\sqrt8=\sqrt{4\times2}.
\]

Split the square root:

\[
\sqrt{4\times2}=\sqrt4\sqrt2.
\]

Since

\[
\sqrt4=2,
\]

we get

\[
\sqrt8=2\sqrt2.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{2\sqrt2}.
\]

---

### Example 26: Simplify \(\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{27}\)

\[
\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{27}
\]

Simplify each surd.

First:

\[
\sqrt{12}=\sqrt{4\times3}=\sqrt4\sqrt3=2\sqrt3.
\]

Second:

\[
\sqrt{27}=\sqrt{9\times3}=\sqrt9\sqrt3=3\sqrt3.
\]

So

\[
\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{27}=2\sqrt3+3\sqrt3.
\]

Collect like surds:

\[
2\sqrt3+3\sqrt3=5\sqrt3.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{5\sqrt3}.
\]

### Like surds behave like like algebra terms

Think of \(\sqrt3\) a bit like a letter.

\[
2\sqrt3+3\sqrt3=5\sqrt3
\]

works like

\[
2x+3x=5x.
\]

---

### Example 27: Expand and simplify \((\sqrt8+1)(\sqrt2-3)\)

\[
(\sqrt8+1)(\sqrt2-3)
\]

Multiply each term in the first bracket by each term in the second:

\[
(\sqrt8)(\sqrt2)+(\sqrt8)(-3)+1(\sqrt2)+1(-3).
\]

So

\[
(\sqrt8+1)(\sqrt2-3)
=
\sqrt{16}-3\sqrt8+\sqrt2-3.
\]

Now simplify:

\[
\sqrt{16}=4.
\]

Also,

\[
\sqrt8=2\sqrt2.
\]

So

\[
-3\sqrt8=-3(2\sqrt2)=-6\sqrt2.
\]

Therefore

\[
\sqrt{16}-3\sqrt8+\sqrt2-3
=
4-6\sqrt2+\sqrt2-3.
\]

Collect rational terms:

\[
4-3=1.
\]

Collect like surds:

\[
-6\sqrt2+\sqrt2=-5\sqrt2.
\]

So

\[
(\sqrt8+1)(\sqrt2-3)
=
1-5\sqrt2.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{1-5\sqrt2}.
\]

---

## 10. Rationalising the Denominator

### Why rationalise?

A denominator such as

\[
\sqrt2
\]

is irrational.

Rationalising the denominator means making the denominator rational while keeping the value of the fraction the same.

The trick is to multiply by \(1\) in a useful form.

For example,

\[
\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}=1.
\]

So multiplying by

\[
\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}
\]

does not change the value.

---

### Example 28: Rationalise \(\dfrac1{\sqrt2}\)

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2}
\]

Multiply top and bottom by \(\sqrt2\):

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2}\times\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}
=
\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2\sqrt2}.
\]

Since

\[
\sqrt2\sqrt2=2,
\]

we get

\[
\frac{\sqrt2}{2}.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}.
\]

---

### Example 29: Rationalise \(\dfrac3{\sqrt2}\)

\[
\frac3{\sqrt2}
\]

Multiply top and bottom by \(\sqrt2\):

\[
\frac3{\sqrt2}\times\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}
=
\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}}.
\]

---

### Example 30: Rationalise \(\dfrac6{\sqrt3}\)

\[
\frac6{\sqrt3}
\]

Multiply top and bottom by \(\sqrt3\):

\[
\frac6{\sqrt3}\times\frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}
=
\frac{6\sqrt3}{3}.
\]

Now simplify the coefficient:

\[
\frac{6\sqrt3}{3}=2\sqrt3.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{2\sqrt3}.
\]

---

### Example 31: Simplify \(\dfrac{4\sqrt2}{\sqrt8}\)

First simplify the denominator:

\[
\sqrt8=2\sqrt2.
\]

So

\[
\frac{4\sqrt2}{\sqrt8}
=
\frac{4\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2}.
\]

Cancel \(\sqrt2\):

\[
\frac{4\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2}
=
\frac42
=
2.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{2}.
\]

### Method note

Although this section teaches rationalising, rationalising is not always the best first move. Sometimes simplifying the surd first is faster.

---

## 11. More Complex Denominators and Conjugates

For a denominator such as

\[
\sqrt2+1,
\]

multiplying by the same expression,

\[
\sqrt2+1,
\]

does not remove the surd.

The denominator would become

\[
(\sqrt2+1)(\sqrt2+1).
\]

Expand:

\[
(\sqrt2)(\sqrt2)+(\sqrt2)(1)+1(\sqrt2)+1(1)
\]

\[
=2+\sqrt2+\sqrt2+1
\]

\[
=3+2\sqrt2.
\]

This still contains a surd.

Instead, multiply by the conjugate:

\[
\sqrt2-1.
\]

The key identity is the difference of two squares:

\[
(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2.
\]

This makes the middle terms cancel.

---

### Example 32: Rationalise \(\dfrac1{\sqrt2+1}\)

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2+1}
\]

Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate:

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2+1}\times\frac{\sqrt2-1}{\sqrt2-1}.
\]

The numerator is

\[
1(\sqrt2-1)=\sqrt2-1.
\]

The denominator is

\[
(\sqrt2+1)(\sqrt2-1).
\]

Use difference of two squares:

\[
(\sqrt2+1)(\sqrt2-1)=(\sqrt2)^2-1^2.
\]

Now

\[
(\sqrt2)^2=2
\]

and

\[
1^2=1.
\]

So

\[
(\sqrt2)^2-1^2=2-1=1.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2+1}\times\frac{\sqrt2-1}{\sqrt2-1}
=
\frac{\sqrt2-1}{1}.
\]

So

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2+1}=\sqrt2-1.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{\sqrt2-1}.
\]

---

### Example 33: Rationalise \(\dfrac3{\sqrt6-2}\)

\[
\frac3{\sqrt6-2}
\]

The conjugate of

\[
\sqrt6-2
\]

is

\[
\sqrt6+2.
\]

Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate:

\[
\frac3{\sqrt6-2}\times\frac{\sqrt6+2}{\sqrt6+2}.
\]

Numerator:

\[
3(\sqrt6+2)=3\sqrt6+6.
\]

Denominator:

\[
(\sqrt6-2)(\sqrt6+2).
\]

Use difference of two squares:

\[
(\sqrt6-2)(\sqrt6+2)=(\sqrt6)^2-2^2.
\]

Now

\[
(\sqrt6)^2=6
\]

and

\[
2^2=4.
\]

So

\[
(\sqrt6)^2-2^2=6-4=2.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac3{\sqrt6-2}
=
\frac{3\sqrt6+6}{2}.
\]

This can be left as

\[
\boxed{\frac{3\sqrt6+6}{2}},
\]

or split:

\[
\frac{3\sqrt6+6}{2}
=
\frac{3\sqrt6}{2}+\frac62
=
\frac{3\sqrt6}{2}+3.
\]

So another form is

\[
\boxed{\frac{3\sqrt6}{2}+3}.
\]

## Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

### Trap 1: Adding powers when you are only collecting terms

Correct:

\[
10x^3+x^3=11x^3.
\]

Incorrect:

\[
10x^3+x^3=11x^6.
\]

You only add indices when multiplying terms with the same base.

---

### Trap 2: Forgetting a negative sign when expanding

In

\[
-x(4-x^2),
\]

you multiply by \(-x\), not \(x\).

\[
-x(4-x^2)=-4x+x^3.
\]

The \(+x^3\) comes from

\[
(-x)(-x^2)=+x^3.
\]

---

### Trap 3: Squaring only part of a bracket

Incorrect:

\[
(2a^3)^2=2a^6.
\]

Correct:

\[
(2a^3)^2=2^2(a^3)^2=4a^6.
\]

---

### Trap 4: Splitting the wrong type of fraction

Correct:

\[
\frac{a+b}{c}=\frac ac+\frac bc.
\]

Incorrect:

\[
\frac{a}{b+c}=\frac ab+\frac ac.
\]

---

### Trap 5: Moving a denominator number to the top

\[
\frac{x^5}{4x^3}
=
\frac14x^2,
\]

not

\[
4x^2.
\]

The \(4\) means “divide by \(4\)”.

---

### Trap 6: Treating \(a^{1/5}\) as division by \(5\)

\[
32^{1/5}
\]

means the fifth root of \(32\), not \(32\div5\).

---

### Trap 7: Writing \(\sqrt9=\pm3\)

\[
\sqrt9=3.
\]

The \(\pm\) appears when solving

\[
x^2=9,
\]

not when simply evaluating \(\sqrt9\).

---

### Trap 8: Rationalising by multiplying by the same binomial

For

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2+1},
\]

multiplying by

\[
\frac{\sqrt2+1}{\sqrt2+1}
\]

does not remove the surd from the denominator.

Use the conjugate:

\[
\frac{\sqrt2-1}{\sqrt2-1}.
\]

---

## Exam Technique

1. **Write powers in descending order** when giving a polynomial answer:
   \[
   11x^3+6x^2-4x
   \]
   is more conventional than
   \[
   6x^2+11x^3-4x.
   \]

2. **Show the rationalising step**, especially in “show that” questions:
   \[
   \frac1{\sqrt2+1}\times\frac{\sqrt2-1}{\sqrt2-1}.
   \]

3. **Use exact forms**, not decimals, unless the question asks for decimals.

4. **When factorising, look for a common factor first.**

5. **For fractional indices, separate the job:**
   - denominator of the fraction means root;
   - numerator of the fraction means power;
   - negative sign means reciprocal.

6. **Do not let the calculator replace the algebra.** The evidence explicitly warns that the point is not merely to get the answer, but to understand and show how it works.

## Full Worked Solutions to Guided Practice

### Solution 1

\[
\left(\frac{2a^5}{a^2}\right)^2\times3a
\]

\[
= (2a^{5-2})^2\times3a
\]

\[
=(2a^3)^2\times3a
\]

\[
=2^2(a^3)^2\times3a
\]

\[
=4a^6\times3a
\]

\[
=12a^{6+1}
\]

\[
=\boxed{12a^7}.
\]

---

### Solution 2

\[
\frac{2x+x^5}{4x^3}
\]

\[
=\frac{2x}{4x^3}+\frac{x^5}{4x^3}
\]

\[
=\frac12x^{1-3}+\frac14x^{5-3}
\]

\[
=\frac12x^{-2}+\frac14x^2.
\]

So

\[
\boxed{\frac12x^{-2}+\frac14x^2}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{\frac{1}{2x^2}+\frac{x^2}{4}}.
\]

---

### Solution 3

\[
2x(3-x^2)-4x^3(3-x)
\]

Expand the first part:

\[
2x(3-x^2)=6x-2x^3.
\]

Expand the second part:

\[
-4x^3(3-x)=-12x^3+4x^4.
\]

So

\[
2x(3-x^2)-4x^3(3-x)
=
6x-2x^3-12x^3+4x^4.
\]

Collect like terms:

\[
-2x^3-12x^3=-14x^3.
\]

Write in descending powers:

\[
\boxed{4x^4-14x^3+6x}.
\]

---

### Solution 4

\[
(x+5)(x-2)(x+1)
\]

First:

\[
(x-2)(x+1)=x^2+x-2x-2=x^2-x-2.
\]

Then:

\[
(x+5)(x^2-x-2)
\]

\[
=x(x^2-x-2)+5(x^2-x-2)
\]

\[
=x^3-x^2-2x+5x^2-5x-10
\]

\[
=x^3+4x^2-7x-10.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{x^3+4x^2-7x-10}.
\]

---

### Solution 5

\[
(2x-1)^3=(2x-1)(2x-1)(2x-1).
\]

First:

\[
(2x-1)(2x-1)=4x^2-4x+1.
\]

Then:

\[
(2x-1)(4x^2-4x+1)
\]

\[
=2x(4x^2-4x+1)-1(4x^2-4x+1)
\]

\[
=8x^3-8x^2+2x-4x^2+4x-1
\]

\[
=8x^3-12x^2+6x-1.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{8x^3-12x^2+6x-1}.
\]

---

### Solution 6

\[
6x^2+x-2
\]

Find two numbers that add to \(1\) and multiply to

\[
6\times(-2)=-12.
\]

The numbers are \(4\) and \(-3\).

\[
6x^2+x-2=6x^2+4x-3x-2.
\]

Factorise by grouping:

\[
6x^2+4x=2x(3x+2)
\]

\[
-3x-2=-1(3x+2).
\]

So

\[
6x^2+4x-3x-2
=
2x(3x+2)-1(3x+2).
\]

Factor out the repeated bracket:

\[
\boxed{(3x+2)(2x-1)}.
\]

---

### Solution 7

\[
x^3-7x^2+12x
\]

First factor out \(x\):

\[
x^3-7x^2+12x=x(x^2-7x+12).
\]

Now factorise the quadratic:

\[
x^2-7x+12=(x-3)(x-4).
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{x(x-3)(x-4)}.
\]

---

### Solution 8

\[
\left(\frac{27}{8}\right)^{-2/3}
\]

Negative power means reciprocal:

\[
\left(\frac{27}{8}\right)^{-2/3}
=
\left(\frac{8}{27}\right)^{2/3}.
\]

Now use cube root then square:

\[
\left(\frac{8}{27}\right)^{2/3}
=
\left(\frac{\sqrt[3]{8}}{\sqrt[3]{27}}\right)^2.
\]

\[
=\left(\frac23\right)^2
\]

\[
=\boxed{\frac49}.
\]

---

### Solution 9

\[
\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{27}
\]

\[
\sqrt{12}=\sqrt{4\times3}=2\sqrt3.
\]

\[
\sqrt{27}=\sqrt{9\times3}=3\sqrt3.
\]

So

\[
\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{27}=2\sqrt3+3\sqrt3=5\sqrt3.
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{5\sqrt3}.
\]

---

### Solution 10

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2+1}
\]

Multiply by the conjugate:

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2+1}\times\frac{\sqrt2-1}{\sqrt2-1}.
\]

\[
=\frac{\sqrt2-1}{(\sqrt2+1)(\sqrt2-1)}.
\]

Use difference of two squares:

\[
(\sqrt2+1)(\sqrt2-1)=(\sqrt2)^2-1^2.
\]

\[
=2-1=1.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2+1}
=
\frac{\sqrt2-1}{1}
=
\boxed{\sqrt2-1}.
\]

## Final Student Checklist

Before moving on, check that you can:

- [ ] Identify the base and index in an expression such as \(3^5\).
- [ ] Use \(a^m a^n=a^{m+n}\).
- [ ] Use \(\dfrac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}\).
- [ ] Use \((a^m)^n=a^{mn}\).
- [ ] Use \((ab)^n=a^nb^n\).
- [ ] Explain why \(a^{-m}=\dfrac1{a^m}\).
- [ ] Explain why \(a^{1/2}=\sqrt a\).
- [ ] Simplify algebraic fractions by splitting over a common denominator.
- [ ] Expand two or three brackets without relying on FOIL.
- [ ] Collect like terms without changing powers.
- [ ] Factorise a quadratic by finding two numbers that add and multiply correctly.
- [ ] Factorise using a common factor first.
- [ ] Recognise and use difference of two squares.
- [ ] Define a surd.
- [ ] Simplify surds such as \(\sqrt8\), \(\sqrt{12}\) and \(\sqrt{27}\).
- [ ] Collect like surds.
- [ ] Rationalise a denominator such as \(\sqrt2\).
- [ ] Use a conjugate to rationalise a denominator such as \(\sqrt2+1\).
- [ ] Show enough working for an examiner to award method marks.
